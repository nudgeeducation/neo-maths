Surface Area

Driving Question: How much material would it take to cover a solid completely?

Lesson type: Measurement — finding the total area of a solid's surface by adding up its faces.

Stepping Stone: calculate the surface area of a solid — the total area of all its faces, for cuboids, prisms, pyramids and cylinders.

🌿 Connection — Add up the faces of the net

🔤 New words for this lesson

Surface area — the total area of the whole outside surface of a solid, measured in square units (cm², m²).
Face — one flat (or curved) surface of the solid.
Net — the solid unfolded flat, showing every face — so its total area is the surface area.

What to do: choose a solid to unfold into its net. Work out the area of each face, type it in, then add them to find the total surface area.

What you are trying to notice: the surface area of a solid is just the total area of its net — every face measured and added, nothing more.

🚶 Movement — Unroll the cylinder's curved side

What to do: drag the unroll slider to peel the curved surface off the can — watch it lift away from the round side and lay itself out flat to the right. Notice what shape it becomes, and how wide it ends up.

What you are trying to notice: the curved surface of a cylinder unrolls into a rectangle — its height is the cylinder's height, and its width is the circle's circumference, 2 π r.

Carry this forward: a cylinder's surface is two circles plus that rectangle: 2 π r² + 2 π r h. Every curved surface can be tamed by unrolling it flat.

🪞 Reflection — Spot the slip

What to do: each card shows a short surface-area calculation. Decide whether it is right or has a slip in it, then reveal the reasoning. These are the mistakes that catch everyone out.

What you are trying to notice: surface area goes wrong in a few predictable ways — a forgotten pair of faces, a diameter used as a radius, a missing face.

🔍 Right or slip?

Carry this forward: checking surface area is really checking that you have counted every face, once each, with the right measurements. Slow and complete beats fast and clever.

🎨 Creativity — The packaging designer

What to do: pick something to wrap or box, read its size, and work out how much material (card or paper) it needs — that is its surface area. Predict, then check.

What you are trying to notice: surface area is a real quantity — the amount of material to cover a shape completely — and a designer works it out every day.

🎁 How much material?

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, design a box for a gift of your choice, sketch its net, and work out how much card it would take.

🌙 Rest — A pause after careful counting

Adding up every face, missing none, is patient and precise work — the kind that rewards slowing down. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: which solid's surface felt easiest to add up, and which made you concentrate hardest? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. This lesson is in every wrapped gift and every cardboard box: surface area is the amount of material needed to cover something.

Why it matters: the paper to wrap a present, the card to make a cereal box, the label around a tin, the paint for a wall — each is a surface area. Designers and manufacturers work it out to use the least material for the job: less waste, lower cost. The careful adding-up you did today is exactly what saves a factory tonnes of cardboard.

Next time you unwrap something, picture its net — how much paper did it really take?

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: find and explain surface areas.

  • Work out the surface area of a cuboid and one other solid (a prism, a pyramid, or a cylinder). Show every face and add them up.
  • Explain, in your own words, why the surface area of a solid is the same as the total area of its net.
  • For a cylinder, explain where the 2 π r h part comes from — what shape is the curved surface when it is unrolled?

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written solution, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
