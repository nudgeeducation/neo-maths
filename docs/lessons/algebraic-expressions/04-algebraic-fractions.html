Simplifying algebraic fractions

Driving Question: When can I cancel, and when can't I?

Lesson type: Core — the rules of number fractions, used with algebra.

Stepping Stone: simplify algebraic fractions by cancelling common factors — numbers, letters and whole brackets; factorise first, then cancel; explain why only factors can be cancelled, never terms; and note the values of x that must be excluded.

You will need: simplifying number fractions, and factorising — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — The number bridge

🔤 The idea for this lesson

To simplify 15/21, write both as products: (3 × 5)/(3 × 7). The common factor 3 cancels, leaving 5/7, because 3/3 = 1. Algebraic fractions work in exactly the same way — but you can only cancel something that multiplies the whole numerator and the whole denominator. A number that is added cannot be cancelled.

What to do: simplify the number fractions, and decide whether each "cancelling" is allowed.

What you are trying to notice: (3 × 5)/3 = 5, but (3 + 5)/3 is not 1 + 5.

🌉 Numbers first

Carry this forward: cancel factors, never terms.

🚶 Movement — Factorise to find the factors

What to do: factorise each expression fully. These are the numerators and denominators you will meet next.

What you are trying to notice: you cannot see what cancels until everything is written as a product.

🔧 Write it as a product

Carry this forward: factorise the numerator and the denominator first — always.

🪞 Reflection — The cancel checker

What to do: Part 1: the numerator and denominator are shown as factor tiles. Tap a factor in the numerator, then the matching factor in the denominator, to cancel them. Then type what is left. Part 2: test some tempting shortcuts. Decide whether each is a valid simplification, then see the evidence.

What you are trying to notice: a whole factor can cancel; a term inside a bracket cannot. And a false cancellation always gives itself away — put in a number and the two sides disagree.

What to do: invent an algebraic fraction, with a quadratic in it, that simplifies to the target. Someone else would have to factorise and cancel to find the target again.

What you are trying to notice: simplifying runs backwards too: multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same bracket.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A long, untidy fraction, and inside it a simple one waiting to be found. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: cancelling is not removing — it is noticing that something divided by itself is 1.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds careful trust in a shortcut. Cancelling is a powerful shortcut — but only when you know exactly why it works. Testing a shortcut with a number before relying on it is a habit worth keeping, in mathematics and beyond.

Carry one idea forward: before you use a shortcut, ask what makes it allowed.

🛠️ Final Task — Factors, not terms

Your task: show that you can simplify algebraic fractions and explain when cancelling is allowed.

  • Simplify fully: 10x³15x, x² + 7x + 10x + 5 and x² − 16x² + 2x − 8. Show the factorised form each time.
  • State any value of x that must be excluded from each, and why.
  • Someone writes x + 88 = x + 1. Use a counterexample to show this is wrong, and explain the mistake.
  • Invent a fraction with a quadratic in it that simplifies to x − 3.
  • stretch Explain, using the idea that a factor over itself is 1, why cancelling a common factor never changes the value of a fraction.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
