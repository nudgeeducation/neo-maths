The circle and its parts

Driving Question: What are all the lines and regions hiding inside a circle?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the circle theorems unit, where a plain circle turns out to be full of named parts and hidden rules.

Stepping Stone: name and identify the parts of a circle — centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, sector, segment, tangent and circumference — and describe the arc that an angle stands on (subtends), the idea every theorem in this unit is built on.

🌿 Connection — A circle is full of named parts

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A circle looks like the plainest shape there is — just a round curve. But hidden inside it is a whole vocabulary, and every circle theorem to come is built from these words. The curve itself is the circumference; its middle is the centre. A straight line from the centre to the curve is a radius; a straight line right across through the centre is a diameter (two radii in a line). Any straight line joining two points on the curve — through the centre or not — is a chord. A piece of the curve itself is an arc. A line that just grazes the circle at a single point is a tangent. And the curve cuts the inside into regions: a wedge between two radii is a sector; the region between a chord and its arc is a segment. Learn to see these, and the rest of the unit has something to stand on.

What to do: read each part's name below the circle, then click that part on the diagram. Work through all nine. A wrong click just nudges you — try again.

What you are trying to notice: some parts are lines (radius, diameter, chord, tangent), some are curves (circumference, arc), and some are regions of the inside (sector, segment). The centre is a single point. Sorting them into those families is half the battle.

🎯 Name that part

Carry this forward: centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, tangent, circumference, sector, segment — the nine words the whole unit is built from.

🚶 Movement — Match the part to its definition

What to do: a circle theorem is only as secure as the words underneath it. Read each definition and choose the one part it describes. Precision matters — a chord is not a diameter unless it passes through the centre.

What you are trying to notice: each part is pinned down by a property, not a picture. "From the centre to the curve" is a radius wherever you draw it; "touches at exactly one point" is a tangent whatever its angle.

🧩 Which part fits the definition?

Carry this forward: know each part by its defining property. That is what lets you spot it in a busy diagram later.

🪞 Reflection — Which arc does an angle stand on?

What to do: two points A and B sit on the circle, with a third point P you can drag around the curve. The angle ∠APB at P is said to stand on (or subtend) an arc. Drag P and watch which arc lights up.

What you are trying to notice: the angle at P stands on the arc AB that does not contain P — the arc "opposite" the angle. Slide P anywhere along its own arc and the arc it stands on never changes: it is always the far arc between A and B. This one idea — the arc an angle stands on — is the hinge of every theorem in this unit.

🔦 The subtending spotlight

Carry this forward: the angle ∠APB subtends (stands on) the arc AB on the far side from P. The same arc AB also subtends an angle at the centre, ∠AOB — a link we open up in the next lesson.

🎨 Creativity — The longest chord, and two kinds of region stretch

What to do: drag the chord up and down. The readout shows its length. Find where the chord is longest. Then use the buttons to shade a sector and a segment and read how they differ.

What you are trying to notice: a chord grows as it slides toward the middle, and it is longest exactly when it passes through the centre — that longest chord is the diameter. A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc (a pizza slice); a segment is bounded by one chord and an arc (a slice off the top).

🧭 Slide the chord — when is it longest?

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, draw a circle and try to draw a chord longer than the diameter. Convince yourself in a sentence why you cannot.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have taken a shape you thought you already knew and found a whole language inside it. That is often how mathematics goes: the familiar turns out to be full of structure once you have the words for it. Take a slow breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: which part was hardest to keep apart from another — chord and diameter, or sector and segment? Naming the confusion is how it clears. There is no right answer here, just what your attention caught.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. Circle geometry is abstract deductive mathematics, so we name honestly what the learning feeds rather than dressing it up as something it is not.

What it feeds: today feeds a taste for precision of language — the quiet power of having exactly the right word, so that "a line from the centre to the edge" becomes simply radius and everyone knows what you mean. And it feeds anticipation: you have just met the idea of an angle standing on an arc, and something tells you that idea is going somewhere. Circles turn up everywhere humans build and navigate — a bridge arch, a camera's field of view, a cog, a wheel — and the vocabulary you are gathering is the shared language engineers and designers reach for.

Carry one idea forward: when a subject feels slippery, the fix is usually to name its parts precisely first.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: produce a fully labelled circle, and show you can tell the parts apart by their properties.

  • Draw a circle, mark the centre O, and clearly label a radius, a diameter, a chord (not through the centre), an arc, a tangent, and the circumference. Shade and label one sector and one segment.
  • In one sentence each, explain the difference between a chord and a diameter, and between a sector and a segment.
  • Mark two points A and B and a third point P on the circle, draw ∠APB, and state which arc the angle stands on (subtends).
  • stretch Explain why the diameter is the longest possible chord.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed hand-drawn labelled circle, a circle built and labelled in the Geometry Studio, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough naming each part.
