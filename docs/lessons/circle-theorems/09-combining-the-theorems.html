NEO Mathematics: Circle Theorems

Combining the theorems

Driving Question: How do you crack a problem that needs several circle theorems at once?

Lesson type: Core — the subtlest theorem of the unit, linking a tangent to an inscribed angle across a chord. Take it slowly.

Stepping Stone: combine the circle theorems in a multi-step angle chase — finding unknown angles one at a time, and naming the theorem that justifies each step.

You will need: that a tangent is perpendicular to the radius (Lesson 6), the angle in a semicircle (Lesson 3), angles in the same segment (Lesson 4), and that angles in a triangle sum to 180°.

🌿 Connection — Six tools, one problem

🔤 The idea for this lesson

You now own a whole toolkit: the angle at the centre, the angle in a semicircle, angles in the same segment, cyclic quadrilaterals, tangents, and the alternate segment theorem. Real problems rarely announce which one to use — and often they need several, in sequence. The skill now is the angle chase: find whatever angle you can, write the reason, and use that new angle to unlock the next. This lesson is about combining the theorems, and — just as importantly — naming the one that justifies each step, because the reasons are what turn a lucky guess into a proof.

What to do: in this figure, AC passes through the centre O, and the central angle ∠AOB = 100° is given. Find the highlighted angle, type its size, and choose the theorem that justifies it.

What you are trying to notice: the very first move in any chase is to look for an angle you can already reach — here, one that stands on the same arc as a known angle.

Carry this forward: start a chase from an angle you can already reach, and name the theorem every time.

🚶 Movement — Which theorem, and what angle?

What to do: each question uses the same figure but gives you a different starting fact. Find the marked angle and, crucially, choose the theorem that justifies your answer.

What you are trying to notice: half the battle is recognising which theorem a situation calls for — a central angle, a diameter, two angles on the same chord, or opposite corners of a cyclic quadrilateral.

🧮 Find the angle, name the theorem

Carry this forward: match the situation to the theorem — a value with the wrong reason is not yet a solution.

🪞 Reflection — The full angle chase put it together

What to do: solve the figure one step at a time. For each highlighted angle, type its size and choose the theorem that justifies it. A correct step is drawn onto the diagram in green and unlocks the next.

What you are trying to notice: each answer you secure becomes a tool for the next step. A three-step chase here uses three different theorems — and every one needs its reason.

🔬 Chase every angle, justify every step

Carry this forward: a multi-theorem problem is cracked one angle at a time, each justified by a named theorem — that chain of reasons is the solution.

🎨 Creativity — Watch a four-theorem chase unfold stretch

What to do: press the steps to watch a complete chase. This one weaves together four theorems in sequence — read the reason at each step and see how each new angle feeds the next.

What you are trying to notice: a long chase is not harder, only longer — the same handful of theorems, applied in turn. The art is choosing a productive first move and keeping the reasons in order.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

This lesson asked you to hold the whole toolkit in mind at once — that is genuinely demanding, so if it felt like a lot, that is because it was. Notice that you did not need any new facts today; you needed to choose among the ones you already have. Take a breath.

Notice one thing: which theorem do you reach for most readily, and which do you tend to forget to look for? Knowing your own blind spot is half of fixing it.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. We name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: combining theorems is strategic thinking — the ability to survey a situation, pick a productive first move, and build a chain of justified steps toward a goal. This is the shape of real mathematics, and of law, medicine, engineering and chess besides: not a single clever trick, but a sequence of small, defensible moves. Today feeds the habit of reasoning in steps you can defend — the difference between an answer that happens to be right and an argument that shows why.

Carry one idea forward: when a problem looks too big, find the one small thing you can work out, and start there.
