NEO Mathematics: Constructions, Loci and Bearings

Shapes that hold their form

Driving Question: Which shapes are fixed by their sides alone?

Lesson type: Core — building triangles and quadrilaterals, and discovering which shapes their sides fix.

Stepping Stone: construct triangles from three given sides and simple quadrilaterals with compass and straightedge, and understand that a triangle is rigid — its three sides fix it — while a quadrilateral can flex unless it is braced.

You will need: the compass idea from Lesson 1 (equal-distance arcs), what a triangle and a quadrilateral are, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator today.

🌿 Connection — Why structures are full of triangles

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Look closely at a bridge, a roof, a crane or an electricity pylon and you will see the same shape again and again: the triangle. That is not decoration. A triangle made from three fixed bars cannot change its shape — engineers call this being rigid. A four-sided frame, by contrast, can lean and squash even when its bars stay the same length. In this lesson you will construct triangles and quadrilaterals for yourself and discover exactly which shapes are fixed by their sides alone.

What to do: here is a simple bridge frame. Press the button to highlight the triangles the engineers built into it.

What you are trying to notice: the frame is braced entirely with triangles. Ask yourself why a builder would choose triangles over squares — that question is the whole lesson.

🌉 Find the triangles

Carry this forward: triangles appear in structures because a triangle made of fixed-length bars holds its shape. Let us find out why.

🚶 Movement — The rigidity detective

What to do: below are two shapes built from bars of fixed length — the bar lengths never change, only the corners can hinge. On the left, a triangle; on the right, a quadrilateral. Take hold of the red corner on each and try to drag it.

What you are trying to notice: the triangle barely moves — its three fixed sides leave it no freedom to change shape. The quadrilateral swings and reshapes freely, even though none of its bars change length. Three sides fix a triangle; four sides do not fix a quadrilateral.

🪞 Reflection — Why does the triangle hold?

What to do: the triangle held its shape and the quadrilateral did not. Here are both again, with their angles shown. Drag the quadrilateral's red corner and watch its angles change; the triangle's angles never do. Then answer the question below.

What you are trying to notice: once you fix three side lengths, the three angles are forced too — there is nothing left to change (this is why "three sides" already told you the whole triangle in Lesson 1). A quadrilateral's four sides leave one angle free to swing, so its shape is not fixed.

So why can the quadrilateral change shape but not the triangle?

Carry this forward: three sides force a triangle's angles, so its shape is fixed. A four-sided shape keeps one degree of freedom, so it can flex.

🎨 Creativity — Brace it! stretch

What to do: engineers turn a floppy quadrilateral into a rigid frame with one clever move. Drag the red corner to see the quadrilateral flex, then press Add a diagonal brace and try to drag it again.

What you are trying to notice: the diagonal splits the quadrilateral into two triangles. Because each triangle is rigid, the whole frame is now locked. This is called triangulation — it is why you see diagonals in gates, shelves and bridges.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have found something engineers rely on every day: three sides make a shape that cannot be pushed out of true. Let that quiet, sturdy idea settle for a moment before moving on.

Notice one thing: where else in the room around you can you spot a triangle doing a structural job — a bracket, a shelf support, a folding chair? There is no need to write anything; just let your attention wander and notice.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment here, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds a feel for why a thing is true, not just that it is — you did not memorise "triangles are rigid", you watched three fixed sides leave no room to move. It also feeds a sense of mathematics as something load-bearing in the real world: the same idea holds up bridges and roofs. Seeing a piece of geometry doing an honest job in the world is one of the most satisfying things learning can offer.

Carry one idea forward: when something must not move, look for the triangle that fixes it.
