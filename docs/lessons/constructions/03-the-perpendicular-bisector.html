NEO Mathematics: Constructions, Loci and Bearings

The perpendicular bisector

Driving Question: Where are all the points that are the same distance from two others?

Lesson type: Core — constructing the perpendicular bisector and meeting it as a locus: every point equidistant from two others.

Stepping Stone: construct the perpendicular bisector of a segment with equal compass arcs, understand it as the locus of points equidistant from the two endpoints, and use it to find a midpoint or a fair point between two places.

You will need: the equal-distance arcs from Lesson 1, the idea of the distance between two points, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator today.

🌿 Connection — The fair meeting point

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Two friends live at A and B and want to meet somewhere exactly the same distance from each home — fair for both. There is not just one fair spot: there is a whole line of them. That line is the perpendicular bisector of AB, and every single point on it is equidistant from A and from B. In this lesson you will discover that line, construct it exactly with a compass, and use it.

What to do: drag the meeting point M until it is the same distance from both homes. The readout shows how far M is from each.

What you are trying to notice: there is more than one fair spot — as you find them, notice they are not scattered randomly; they line up.

🏠 Find a fair spot

Carry this forward: a point the same distance from A and B is equidistant from them — and the fair spots line up.

🚶 Movement — Every equidistant point

What to do: drag the point P anywhere. The readouts show PA and PB. Whenever they are equal, P turns green and drops a mark. Hunt for equal-distance points all over the plane.

What you are trying to notice: the green marks do not fill the plane — they fall on a single straight line that cuts AB in half at a right angle. That line is the perpendicular bisector: the set of all points equidistant from A and B.

🎯 Drag P — collect the equal-distance points

Carry this forward: the perpendicular bisector of AB is every point the same distance from A and from B. It is perpendicular to AB and passes through its midpoint.

🪞 Reflection — Constructing it exactly (and why it works)

What to do: the compass builds this line exactly. Follow the steps: swing an equal arc from A and from B (wider than half of AB), mark the two crossings, and join them.

What you are trying to notice: both arcs have the same radius, so each crossing is the same distance from A and from B — each crossing is an equidistant point. Two equidistant points are enough to fix the whole line. That is why the join is the perpendicular bisector.

🧭 The construction

Carry this forward: equal arcs from A and B cross at points equidistant from both; the line through those crossings is the perpendicular bisector — perpendicular to AB, through its midpoint.

🎨 Creativity — A circle through both points stretch

What to do: the perpendicular bisector is drawn as a dashed line. Drag the centre O up and down along it, and watch the circle centred at O through A.

What you are trying to notice: wherever O sits on the bisector, the circle also passes through B — because O is equidistant from A and B, so OA and OB are equal radii. Every circle through both A and B has its centre on this line.

⭕ Slide the centre along the bisector

Make it your own: in the Studio, put a point on the bisector and draw a circle through A — then drag it and watch B stay on the circle.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A single, simple question — "where is it fair?" — turned into a whole line, and then into an exact construction. That is mathematics quietly widening a question. Take a breath and let it settle.

Notice one thing: did it surprise you that "the same distance from two points" makes a straight line, rather than a curve or a single spot? There is no right answer — just what your attention noticed.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the idea of a locus — a shape defined by a rule rather than drawn by hand ("all points such that…"). That is a genuinely powerful way of thinking that runs right through mathematics. It also feeds the quiet pleasure of a fair answer: the perpendicular bisector is the honest, balanced line between two points, and there is something satisfying about finding it exactly rather than guessing.

Carry one idea forward: when a question asks "all the points that…", you are looking for a locus.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today
