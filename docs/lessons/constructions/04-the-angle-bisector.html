NEO Mathematics: Constructions, Loci and Bearings

The angle bisector

Driving Question: How can I split any angle into two exactly equal halves?

Lesson type: Core — constructing the angle bisector and meeting it as a locus: every point equidistant from the two arms of an angle.

Stepping Stone: construct the bisector of an angle with compass and straightedge, understand it as the locus of points equidistant from the two arms of the angle, and use it to split an angle exactly or to find a circle that touches both arms.

You will need: the equal-distance arcs from Lesson 1, the perpendicular-bisector idea from Lesson 3, the meaning of an angle, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — The equal-distance path

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Two straight roads meet at a junction V. You want to lay a path that stays the same distance from both roads as it heads away from the junction. There is exactly one such path, and it runs straight up the middle of the angle — it is the angle bisector. Every point on it is equidistant from the two arms of the angle. In Lesson 3 you found the points equidistant from two points; this lesson you find the points equidistant from two lines.

What to do: drag the point P until it is the same distance from each road. The readout shows the perpendicular distance to each arm.

What you are trying to notice: there is more than one fair spot — and, just like last lesson, they line up along a single line.

🛣️ Find a spot equally far from both roads

Carry this forward: a point the same distance from both arms is equidistant from them — and the equal-distance spots line up.

🚶 Movement — Every equidistant point

What to do: drag P around inside the angle. The readouts show its perpendicular distance to arm 1 and to arm 2. Whenever they are equal, P turns green and drops a mark.

What you are trying to notice: the green marks fall on a single straight line running from the vertex up the middle of the angle. That line is the angle bisector: the set of all points equidistant from the two arms — and it splits the angle into two equal halves.

🎯 Drag P — collect the equal-distance points

Carry this forward: the angle bisector is every point equidistant from the two arms. It runs from the vertex and cuts the angle exactly in half.

🪞 Reflection — Constructing it, and the fold that explains it

What to do: follow the construction: swing an arc from the vertex V to cut both arms at P and Q; then swing equal arcs from P and from Q; join V to where they cross. Then press Fold to see the symmetry.

What you are trying to notice: because VP = VQ and the two arcs from P and Q are equal, the whole picture is symmetrical about the line you drew — it is the fold line that lays one arm exactly onto the other. That symmetry is why the two halves are equal and every point on the line is equidistant from both arms.

🧭 The construction

Carry this forward: equal arcs make the picture symmetrical about V–R; the bisector is the fold line that maps one arm onto the other, so it halves the angle exactly.

🎨 Creativity — A circle touching both arms stretch

What to do: the bisector is drawn as a dashed line. Drag the centre O along it, and watch the circle centred at O that just touches arm 1.

What you are trying to notice: wherever O sits on the bisector, the circle also just touches arm 2 — because O is the same distance from both arms, so that distance is the radius to each. Every circle sitting snugly inside the angle has its centre on the bisector.

⭕ Slide the centre along the bisector

Make it your own: in the Studio, put a point on the bisector and draw a circle that touches one arm — then drag it and watch it stay touching the other arm too.

📐 Build the circle in both arms

Construct the bisector, add a centre on it, drop a perpendicular to one arm for the radius, and draw a circle that touches both arms.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Two lessons, one idea, gently turned: "the same distance from two things" made a line each time — once from two points, once from two arms. That is a quiet kind of pattern worth noticing. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: did you spot that this lesson rhymed with the last one? Equidistant from two points gave a line; equidistant from two arms gave a line too. There is no need to write anything — just let the echo land.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the pleasure of a pattern across ideas — seeing that "equidistant" makes a line whether you measure from points or from lines deepens the sense that mathematics is connected, not a pile of separate tricks. It also feeds the elegance of symmetry: the bisector is simply the fold that makes an angle match itself, and there is something satisfying in a construction that works because the picture is balanced.

Carry one idea forward: symmetry is often the quiet reason a construction is exact.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: construct the bisector of an angle and show that it halves it.

  • Draw an angle, then construct its bisector: an arc from the vertex to cut both arms, equal arcs from those two points, and a line from the vertex through the crossing. Leave your arcs showing.
  • Check the two halves are equal — by measuring, or by folding along the bisector.
  • Explain in a sentence what every point on the angle bisector is equidistant from.
  • stretch Put a point on your bisector and draw a circle that touches both arms of the angle.
