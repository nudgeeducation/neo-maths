NEO Mathematics: Constructions, Loci and Bearings

Loci — the shape of a rule

Driving Question: What shape do all the points obeying a rule make?

Lesson type: Core — meeting the idea of a locus and constructing the basic loci: a fixed distance from a point, a fixed distance from a line, and regions from two rules.

Stepping Stone: understand a locus as the set of all points obeying a rule; construct the loci a fixed distance from a point (a circle) and a fixed distance from a line (a rounded 'racetrack'); and combine two rules to find a region.

You will need: the two loci you already met — the perpendicular bisector (Lesson 3) and the angle bisector (Lesson 4) — the idea of distance, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — Where can the goat reach?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A goat is tied to a post by a rope in an open field. It can wander, but only so far — the rope decides where it can and cannot reach. The set of all the places it can stand is a shape, and that shape is fixed by one simple rule: "no further than the rope from the post." A set of all the points obeying a rule like this is called a locus (plural loci). You have already met two loci in this unit — the perpendicular bisector and the angle bisector. This lesson names the idea and builds the everyday ones.

What to do: drag the goat to change the rope length. The shaded region is everywhere it can reach.

What you are trying to notice: the edge of where it can reach is a perfect circle, and the region it can graze is the disc inside. "A fixed distance from a point" makes a circle.

🐐 The grazing region

Carry this forward: the locus of points a fixed distance from a point is a circle; "within that distance" fills the disc.

🚶 Movement — The shape of a rule

What to do: choose a rule, then drag the point P. Whenever P is exactly the target distance from the chosen thing, it turns green and drops a mark. Collect the marks all over the plane.

What you are trying to notice: the marks are not scattered — they trace out a definite shape, the locus of that rule. A fixed distance from the point O gives a circle; a fixed distance from the segment AB gives a rounded 'racetrack' — two straight sides with semicircular ends.

🎯 Pick a rule and drag P

Carry this forward: a locus is the set of all points obeying a rule. Fixed distance from a point → a circle; fixed distance from a segment → straight sides with semicircular ends.

🪞 Reflection — Why the racetrack has round ends

What to do: look at the locus a fixed distance from the segment AB. Press each button to see where a point on the straight part, and a point on the curved end, gets its distance from.

What you are trying to notice: beside the segment, the nearest point is straight across — so the locus runs parallel to AB. But beyond an end, the nearest point of the segment is the endpoint itself, so those points are a fixed distance from a single point — an arc. That is why the ends are semicircles.

🔎 Where is each point's distance measured from?

Carry this forward: the locus a fixed distance from a segment is two parallel straight parts joined by two semicircular ends (each an arc a fixed distance from an endpoint).

🎨 Creativity — The safe zone: two rules at once stretch

What to do: real problems combine rules. Turn each condition on and off and watch the shaded region — the points that satisfy both at once.

What you are trying to notice: each rule has its own boundary (a circle, or the perpendicular bisector), and the answer is the region where they overlap. This is how "safe zones", signal-coverage and catchment problems are solved.

🛡️ Shade what satisfies both

Make it your own: in the Studio, construct the two boundaries — a circle and a perpendicular bisector — and identify the overlap region.

📐 Build a region from two rules

Construct a circle centred A and the perpendicular bisector of AB, then find the region that is inside the circle and on A's side of the bisector.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A goat on a rope, a signal from a mast, the fair line between two towns — all the same idea, quietly: a rule, and the shape it makes. Take a breath and let that one idea hold the whole lesson together.

Notice one thing: where else in the world is a shape really just "everywhere that obeys a rule" — the reach of a sprinkler, a Wi-Fi zone, a coastline's safe distance? There is no need to write anything; just let your attention wander and notice.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds one of the most powerful moves in mathematics — defining a shape by a rule instead of drawing it by hand ("all the points such that…"). Once you can think that way, circles, regions and safe zones stop being things to memorise and become things you can derive. It also feeds a quiet sense of usefulness: loci are how engineers, planners and designers describe where something can and cannot be.

Carry one idea forward: when a question describes a rule about distance, you are being asked for a locus.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: construct loci and use them to find a region.

  • Construct the locus of points a fixed distance from a point (a circle) and describe the locus a fixed distance from a segment (straight sides, semicircular ends).
  • Solve a region problem: shade the points that are within a given distance of A and closer to A than to B (a circle and a perpendicular bisector).
  • Explain in a sentence what the word locus means.
  • stretch Describe the locus of points equidistant from two crossing lines (a pair of angle bisectors).
