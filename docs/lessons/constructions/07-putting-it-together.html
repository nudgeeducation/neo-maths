NEO Mathematics: Constructions, Loci and Bearings

Putting it together — scale plans and compound constructions

Driving Question: How do constructions, loci and bearings work together to solve a real plan?

Lesson type: Capstone — combining everything in the unit on a scale plan: choosing the right construction for each rule and shading the region that satisfies them all.

Stepping Stone: work at a given scale (1 cm represents n m); choose the right locus for each rule; and combine several constraints on a scale plan to find and shade the region that satisfies them all.

You will need: everything from this unit — bisectors (Lessons 3–4), loci and regions (Lesson 5), bearings (Lesson 6) — plus dividing to convert with a scale, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — A plan stands in for a place

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A map or a plan is a shrunk copy of the real world, drawn to a fixed scale — for example 1 cm : 5 m, meaning every 1 cm on the plan stands for 5 m in real life. That single ratio lets you measure a length on the paper and turn it into a real distance, and turn a real distance into a length to draw. In this final lesson you will use a scale, together with everything you have built — bisectors, loci, regions and bearings — to solve a real plan.

What to do: drag the end of the measuring line across the plan. The readout shows both the plan length (cm) and the real distance (m).

What you are trying to notice: to go from plan to real you multiply by the scale (×5 here); to go from real to plan you divide (÷5). The plan and the place are the same shape, just a fixed number of times smaller.

📏 Measure on the plan

Carry this forward: at a scale of 1 cm : 5 m — plan → real means × 5; real → plan means ÷ 5.

🚶 Movement — The allowed region

What to do: here is a plan at 1 cm : 5 m. Turn each rule on or off and watch the shaded region — the places that obey every rule you have switched on.

What you are trying to notice: each rule is a locus you already know — a circle (a fixed distance from a point), a perpendicular bisector (closer to one point than another), a parallel band (a distance from a fence). The answer is always the region where they overlap. This is how a real siting problem is solved.

🗺️ Shade what obeys every rule

Carry this forward: a compound plan problem = draw each rule's boundary (a locus), then shade the overlap that satisfies them all.

🪞 Reflection — Which locus for which rule?

What to do: the whole unit gave you a toolkit of loci. Read each rule and choose the construction that draws it. A small diagram of your choice appears so you can check it.

What you are trying to notice: every "where can it be?" rule matches one locus — equidistant from two points → perpendicular bisector; equidistant from two lines → angle bisector; a fixed distance from a point → circle; a fixed distance from a line → a parallel band. Naming the right tool is the heart of a plan problem.

🧰 Match the rule to the locus

Carry this forward: reading the rule tells you the locus: two points → perpendicular bisector; two lines → angle bisector; one point → circle; one line → parallel band.

🎨 Creativity — Solve a plan from a brief stretch

What to do: here is a real brief. Build each rule's boundary in turn, then reveal the region where the new bench is allowed.

Brief. A park (plan scale 1 cm : 5 m). A new bench must be:
• equidistant from the two straight paths that meet at the gate, and
• no more than 20 m from the fountain F.
Where can the bench go?

🪑 Build it, step by step

Make it your own: in the Studio, construct the angle bisector of the two paths and a circle around F, and find where they overlap.

📐 Build the plan to scale

Build a circle to a scale distance and check it with Measure → Length, then combine it with a bisector to solve a plan.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have reached the end of a whole unit. Two humble tools — a straightedge and a pair of compasses — grew into a way of describing exactly where things can and cannot be. That is a real journey. Take a breath and let the whole of it settle for a moment.

Notice one thing: which idea from these seven lessons felt most surprising or most useful to you — the exactness of a construction, the shape of a rule, or a direction pinned down by a number? There is no right answer; just notice what stayed with you.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the satisfaction of synthesis — taking separate ideas you have built one at a time and using them together to solve something real. That is what mathematics is for: not isolated tricks, but a connected toolkit you reach into. It also feeds a sense of agency — a plan is a decision made exactly, and you now have the tools to make and defend one.

Carry one idea forward: a hard problem is usually several familiar ones, chosen well and combined.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for the unit

Your task: solve a compound scale-plan problem from start to finish.

  • Choose a scale and convert the real distances in the brief into plan lengths (divide by the scale).
  • Choose the right construction for each rule — circle, perpendicular bisector, angle bisector, or a distance from a line.
  • Build every boundary accurately, then shade the region that satisfies all the rules.
  • Name the locus behind each rule, and state the scale you used.
  • stretch Add a bearing constraint (for example, "on a bearing between 060° and 120° from the gate") and shade the smaller region.
