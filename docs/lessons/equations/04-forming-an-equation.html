NEO Mathematics: Equations, Inequalities and Simultaneous Relationships

Forming an equation

Driving Question: How do I turn a situation into an equation I can solve?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the algebra unit, where solving becomes keeping the balance.

Stepping Stone: form a linear equation from a worded or geometric situation by naming the unknown and writing what the situation says, then solve it and interpret the answer back in the context, checking it makes the story true.

You will need: the four operations and their inverses, working with negative numbers, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed today.

🌿 Connection — Turn the words into an equation

🔤 The idea for this lesson

An equation is a question in disguise. When a situation asks "what number makes this work?", you can capture it exactly: name the unknown (let x be the thing you don't know), then write what the situation says as an equation. Once it is written, you already know how to solve it — and the final move is to check the answer against the story, not just the algebra.

What to do: read the situation, then build its equation by filling in the multiplier, the number added or subtracted, and the result. Press Check, then see it solved and tested against the story.

What you are trying to notice: the words map straight onto the equation — "multiply by 3" is the 3 in front of x, "add 4" is + 4, "the result is 19" is = 19. Forming is just careful translation.

🚶 Movement — See it as a bar

What to do: a bar model lays a situation out as parts of a whole. Each x block is the unknown; number blocks are known amounts; the whole bar is the total. Read the equation straight off the bar, then solve it.

What you are trying to notice: the bar makes the equation obvious — count the x blocks (the coefficient), add the number block, set it equal to the total. Then it is a two-step equation you already know.

📊 Read the bar, solve the equation

Carry this forward: a bar model turns a story into an equation you can see, then solve.

🪞 Reflection — Solve, then answer the real question

What to do: here the equation is already formed. Solve it, then read the situation again — because the question often asks for something other than x (a sister's age, the larger number, a length). Give the answer the story actually wants.

What you are trying to notice: solving finds x; interpreting turns x back into the answer the situation asked for. An unchecked or uninterpreted answer is only half the job.

🔎 From x back to the story

Carry this forward: solve for x, then interpret — answer what the situation actually asked, and check it fits.

🎨 Creativity — Write your own puzzle stretch

What to do: forming is just doing to a chosen number. Pick the answer you want, choose what to do to it, and watch your own worded puzzle appear — one whose answer you already know.

What you are trying to notice: every "think of a number" puzzle is built forwards from its answer, so it can always be solved backwards to it. Writing one is the exact reverse of solving one.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

There is something quietly powerful about turning a sentence into an equation: a everyday situation, captured exactly in symbols, and then answered for certain. Words become maths, maths becomes an answer, and the answer speaks back to the words. Take a breath.

Notice one thing: did naming the unknown ("let x be…") make a woolly problem suddenly feel handleable? That single move is often the hardest and most useful step. Just notice how it felt.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: contextual. Forming equations genuinely lives in everyday budgeting and planning, so here the context is honest — not a disguise for abstract algebra.

What it feeds: the skill in this lesson is exactly what makes a budget or a plan work out. "A bus pass is £15 plus £2 a journey — how many journeys before it's worth £29?" is a formed equation. Being able to name an unknown and write the situation down puts you in control of real decisions about money, time and resources, instead of guessing.

Carry one idea forward: when a real choice depends on an unknown amount, name it x and write the situation as an equation — then solve for the answer.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: form equations from situations, solve them, and interpret the answers.

  • Solve at least three worded problems: a "think of a number" puzzle, a money/cost situation, and one where the question asks for something other than x (an age, a length, the larger number).
  • For each, name the unknown, write the equation, solve it, then interpret the answer in the situation.
  • Check each answer makes the original story true.
  • stretch Write your own worded puzzle with a chosen answer, then hand it to someone to solve back.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed hand-written solution showing each line, a short spoken walkthrough, or a poster of one problem from story to answer.
