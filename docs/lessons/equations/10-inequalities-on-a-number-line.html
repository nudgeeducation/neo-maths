Inequalities on a number line

Driving Question: What if “equals” becomes “at least” or “at most”?

🌿 Connection — Not one answer, but a range

🔤 The idea for this lesson

An equation like x = 3 has one answer. But "you must be at least 12 to ride" or "spend at most £10" describes a whole range of allowed values. That is an inequality. We write > (more than), < (less than), ≥ (at least / greater than or equal to) and ≤ (at most / less than or equal to), and we picture the solution on a number line. A closed circle (●) means the end value is included; an open circle (○) means it is not.

What to do: switch between the four kinds of inequality at the value 2 and watch the number line change.

What you are trying to notice: the direction of the arrow shows which values are allowed, and the circle (open or closed) shows whether the boundary itself counts.

➡️ Read a range on the line

Carry this forward: an inequality's solution is a range: the arrow gives the direction, the circle (○ open / ● closed) gives whether the boundary is included.

🚶 Movement — Solve it like an equation (and see the one difference)

What to do: first see why multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses an inequality — it is not a rule to memorise, it is something you can watch happen. Take a true statement like 2 < 5 and multiply both sides by −1: every number reflects across 0, and the order swaps. Then solve inequalities using that understanding.

What you are trying to notice: multiplying by −1 flips the whole number line about 0 — small positives become large-looking negatives. So if 2 was less than 5, then −2 is more than −5. The inequality sign must reverse simply to keep the statement true.

🪞 Why the sign reverses

The two sides reflect across 0 — see how the smaller value becomes the larger. That is the whole reason the sign flips.

✍️ Solve the inequality

Carry this forward: solve just like an equation — and because a negative multiplier reflects both sides across 0, the inequality reverses whenever you multiply or divide by a negative.

🪞 Reflection — Solve, then draw it

What to do: solve the inequality, then represent your answer: enter the boundary value and choose the relation. The number line draws itself — check the circle and the direction match your solution.

What you are trying to notice: the symbolic answer and the picture say the same thing. > and < give an open circle (boundary excluded); ≥ and ≤ give a closed circle (boundary included). The relation points the arrow.

Carry this forward: > < → open circle; ≥ ≤ → closed circle; the relation sets the arrow's direction.

🎨 Creativity — Trapped between two bounds stretch

What to do: sometimes a value is squeezed between two limits at once, like 1 ≤ x < 4. Solve the double inequality by doing the same to all three parts, and picture the result as a segment between two ends.

What you are trying to notice: a double inequality is two conditions in one line. Its solution is the stretch of the number line between the bounds, with each end open or closed on its own.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Most of life is not "exactly this" but "at least" or "no more than" — enough sleep, within budget, over the pass mark. An inequality is just a calm, precise way of saying "anywhere in this range is fine." There is a kind of freedom in that. Take a breath.

Notice one thing: did an answer that is a whole range feel looser than a single number — less like one right answer, more like a set of allowed ones? Just notice the shift.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment here, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds a feel for constraints and limits — the everyday logic of "at least", "at most", "within". Real decisions rarely have a single right answer; they have a range of acceptable ones, and being able to describe and picture that range exactly is quietly powerful, from budgets to safety margins to fair thresholds.

Carry one idea forward: an answer can be a whole range — and a number line shows exactly where the boundary is, and whether it counts.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: solve inequalities and represent their solutions on number lines.

  • Solve at least three inequalities, showing each step. Include one where you must flip the sign (dividing or multiplying by a negative).
  • For each, draw the solution on a number line with the correct open or closed end and arrow direction.
  • Say in words what the range means (for example "x is at most 3").
