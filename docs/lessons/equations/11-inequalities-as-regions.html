NEO Mathematics: Equations, Inequalities and Simultaneous Relationships

Inequalities as regions

Driving Question: Where do all the allowed points live on a graph?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the algebra unit, where solving becomes keeping the balance.

Stepping Stone: represent a linear inequality in two variables as a region of the plane — a boundary line (dashed for strict, solid for inclusive) with the correct side shaded — and identify the region satisfying one or two inequalities at once.

You will need: the four operations and their inverses, working with negative numbers, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed today.

🌿 Connection — A line splits the plane in two

🔤 The idea for this lesson

On a number line, an inequality was a range. On a graph, an inequality in two variables is a whole region of the plane. The boundary is a line — dashed if the inequality is strict (< or >, the boundary is not included) and solid if it is inclusive (≤ or ≥). The line splits the plane into two sides; the inequality's solutions are all the points on one side. To find which side, test a point — the origin (0, 0) is easiest — and shade the side that makes the inequality true.

What to do: look at each inequality's region: the boundary style, and which side is shaded. The origin is marked so you can see the test.

What you are trying to notice: every point in the shaded region satisfies the inequality, and no point outside it does. The boundary line is the borderline case — included (solid) or excluded (dashed).

🟩 See the region

Carry this forward: boundary dashed (< >) or solid (≤ ≥); test a point to choose the side; shade all the solutions.

🚶 Movement — Which side satisfies it?

What to do: the boundary is drawn and the origin is marked. Decide which side to shade by testing whether the origin satisfies the inequality — then choose the side with the origin or the other side.

What you are trying to notice: one test point settles the whole region. If the origin makes the inequality true, its side is the region; if not, the other side is. You never have to check more than one point.

📍 Test a point, pick the side

Carry this forward: test one point (the origin if you can); shade its side if it works, the other side if it does not.

🪞 Reflection — Draw the region, step by step

What to do: build the region yourself. Decide the boundary style (dashed or solid), test the origin, then choose the side to shade. Get all three right and the region appears.

What you are trying to notice: the whole method is three small decisions — strictness sets the boundary style, a single test point sets the side, and the region is every solution at once. Each decision has a clear reason behind it.

🎯 Shade the region

Carry this forward: strict → dashed, inclusive → solid; test the origin; shade the side that satisfies it.

🎨 Creativity — Where two regions overlap stretch

What to do: two inequalities must both hold. Each is a region; the points satisfying both are where the two regions overlap — exactly the "region" idea from the Constructions unit, now made from straight boundaries.

What you are trying to notice: the overlap is the set of points obeying every condition at once — the same reasoning as a compound locus. Shade each region, and the answer is the part they share.

🔗 Shade the overlap

🌙 Rest — A pause to close the unit

Look how far the idea of a "solution" has travelled this unit: from a single number, to a pair, to a whole line of pairs, to two lines meeting at one point, and now to entire regions of the plane. Same word, ever-widening meaning. That is mathematics quietly growing more powerful. Take a breath — you have come a long way.

Notice one thing: which moment in this unit shifted your sense of what an "answer" can be? A single value, a crossing, a range, a region? Just notice where it stretched.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment here, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds a way of seeing all the allowed possibilities at once — a region is every acceptable choice, drawn in one picture. This is how real constraints combine: a budget and a time limit and a minimum standard carve out the set of workable options. Linear programming, timetabling, and design-within-limits all begin exactly here, with shaded regions and their overlaps.

Carry one idea forward: when several conditions must all hold, the answer is not a point but a region — the overlap of everything allowed.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today (and the unit)

Your task: represent inequalities as regions, and draw the unit together.

  • Draw the region for at least three inequalities: one like y > x + 1, one like x ≤ 2, and one you choose. Use the correct dashed/solid boundary and shade the right side (show your test point).
  • Shade the overlap of two inequalities and describe the region in words.
  • Check by picking a point inside your region and one outside, and testing both.
  • stretch In a sentence each, say how "solution" has changed across the unit: one value → a pair → a line → an intersection → a range → a region.

Submission choices: annotated graphs with shaded regions and test points, a Scratchpad image, a short spoken walkthrough, or a poster tracing "what a solution can be" across the unit.
