NEO Mathematics: Number Systems and Calculator Judgement

Order of operations and calculator judgement

Driving Question: When does the calculator get it wrong — and when do I?

Lesson type: Core — the order of operations with powers, roots, negative numbers and fraction bars, and keying calculations so a calculator does what you mean.

Stepping Stone: apply the order of operations to calculations with powers, roots, negative numbers and fraction bars; explain why −3² = −9 but (−3)² = 9; use brackets so a calculator works out what was written; and judge whether a calculator's answer is sensible.

You will need: the order of operations, squares and square roots — and any calculator, if you have one. Today the calculator is part of the lesson. The optional ✏️ Scratchpad is there too.

🌿 Connection — First things first

🔤 The idea for this lesson

The order of operations tells everyone — people and calculators — which part of a calculation to do first: brackets, then powers and roots, then × and ÷ (left to right), then + and − (left to right). A fraction bar and a √ sign group things too, just like brackets. A calculator follows these rules exactly. It does what it is asked — which is not always what was meant.

What to do: for each calculation, choose the value. Then see the order it is worked out in.

What you are trying to notice: where a calculation is written with a power, a √ sign or a fraction bar, something is grouped without brackets being written.

🔢 Which value is right?

Carry this forward: brackets first; then powers and roots; then × and ÷ from left to right; then + and − from left to right. A fraction bar and a √ sign group like brackets.

🚶 Movement — What the calculator heard

What to do: each calculation below has already been keyed in, exactly as it looks. Press = to see what the calculator heard — drawn as it would be written — and the order it worked in. If it heard something different, change the keys (use DEL or AC) and add brackets until it hears what is written.

What you are trying to notice: a fraction bar groups the whole numerator and the whole denominator; a √ sign reaches over everything under it; a power belongs only to what is just before it. When you key a calculation in a line, those groups must be typed as brackets.

This on-screen calculator's √ applies only to the number or bracket straight after it, and any missing ) at the end is added for you. Real calculators differ a little — many have a fraction key, or open a bracket after √. Whatever yours does, check that the display shows what you meant.

🪞 Reflection — Can I trust it?

What to do: each calculation has been put into a calculator. Before you believe the display, estimate. Is the answer sensible — or was something keyed in wrongly? If so, which group was missing its brackets?

What you are trying to notice: a quick estimate, or a simple fact (a square is never negative; √ means the positive square root), is often enough to catch an entry slip.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A calculator never gets tired and never guesses. It simply does what it is told. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: the order of operations is an agreement — a shared language that lets everyone, and every machine, read the same calculation in the same way.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds trust in your own judgement. A tool is powerful when you know what it is doing — and when you can tell, with a quick estimate, that something has gone wrong. That is true of calculators, spreadsheets and every machine that gives you an answer.

Carry one idea forward: you are in charge of the calculator, not the other way round.

🛠️ Final Task — Keys that say what you mean

Your task: show that you can apply the order of operations, key calculations correctly, and judge a calculator's answer.

  • Without a calculator, work out −2⁴, (−2)⁴ and 30 − 12 ÷ 3 × 2. Explain the order you used for each.
  • Write the keys you would press for 5.4 + 2.61.2 × 0.5 and for √13² − 5². Check both with a calculator.
  • Someone gets 57.5 for 45 + 52 × 5. Estimate the answer, explain the slip, and give the correct answer.
  • Write a calculation of your own where leaving out the brackets changes the answer. Show both answers.
  • stretch Find a calculation where leaving out the brackets does not change the answer. Explain why.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
