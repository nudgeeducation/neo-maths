NEO Mathematics: Number Systems and Calculator Judgement

Surds: manipulating and rationalising

Driving Question: How do I tidy a surd out of the denominator of a fraction?

Lesson type: Core — expanding brackets with surds, and using what you find to clear a surd from a denominator.

Stepping Stone: multiply surds and expand brackets containing surds; notice that (a + √b)(a − √b) has no surd left; and rationalise the denominator of fractions such as 1/√2 and 4/(3 + √5), explaining why the value does not change.

You will need: expanding brackets, and simplifying surds — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator today: exact answers are the point.

🌿 Connection — Surds times surds

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A surd multiplied by itself gives a whole number: √3 × √3 = 3. That one fact lets us expand brackets with surds — and then do something useful: move a surd out of the denominator of a fraction. This is called rationalising the denominator, because the denominator becomes rational (a whole number or fraction).

What to do: work out each product exactly. Use the √ Symbols button to type √.

What you are trying to notice: multiply the whole numbers together and the surds together. A surd times the same surd is a whole number.

✖️ Multiply

Carry this forward: √b × √b = b. Whole numbers multiply whole numbers; surds multiply surds.

🚶 Movement — The surd expansion grid

What to do: fill in each cell of the grid: the term at the start of its row times the term at the head of its column. Press Check cells. Then collect like terms — whole numbers together, surds together.

What you are trying to notice: in some grids, the two surd cells are equal and opposite. They cancel — and the answer is a whole number. Look at what those brackets have in common.

stretch Why must (a + √b)(a − √b) have no surd? A pattern suggests — a proof convinces.

Carry this forward: (a + √b) and (a − √b) are called conjugates. Multiplied together they give a² − b: no surd.

🪞 Reflection — Clear the denominator

Why bother? 1/√2 and √2/2 are the same number. But before calculators, working out 1 ÷ 1.41421… by hand was hard, while 1.41421… ÷ 2 was easy. Today, a rationalised form is the agreed tidy form: it makes answers easy to compare and to collect.

What to do: choose what to multiply the numerator and denominator by. Then work out the new denominator, and the tidy answer.

What you are trying to notice: multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same thing is multiplying by 1 — so the value never changes. For √b, use √b. For a + √b, use its conjugate a − √b.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Two messy-looking brackets, and everything awkward cancels away. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: the two surd cells in a conjugate grid are mirror images: one adds exactly what the other takes away.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the pleasure of a trick that turns out to be a truth. Multiplying by the conjugate looks like a clever trick — but it is just the difference of two squares, a² − b, which you proved. Understanding why a method works means you never have to remember it blindly.

Carry one idea forward: when a method seems like magic, look for the identity hiding underneath.

🛠️ Final Task — Tidy denominators

Your task: show that you can expand brackets with surds and rationalise denominators, and explain why it works.

  • Expand and simplify (3 + √2)(1 + √2) and (√7 + 2)(√7 − 2).
  • Rationalise the denominators: 9√3 and 64 − √10.
  • Explain why multiplying by 4 + √104 + √10 does not change the value of a fraction.
  • Show that 1√2 + 1 = √2 − 1.
  • stretch Prove that (a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b, and explain why this always clears the surd from a denominator like a + √b.
