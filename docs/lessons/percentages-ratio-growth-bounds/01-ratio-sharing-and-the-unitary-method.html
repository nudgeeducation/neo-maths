Ratio, sharing and the unitary method

Driving Question: How can one part tell me everything?

Lesson type: Core — recipes as ratios, and food labels read by finding one, then scaling.

Stepping Stone: write and simplify ratios, share a quantity in a given ratio, and use the unitary method: find one, then scale.

You will need: highest common factors, a fraction of an amount, and money in pounds and pence, plus the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — The Recipe Bar

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A ratio compares amounts in parts. A smoothie made as fruit : yoghurt : milk = 2 : 1 : 1 has 4 equal parts: 2 of fruit, 1 of yoghurt and 1 of milk. To share a batch in a ratio, find the size of one part first. That is the unitary method: find one, then scale. Multiplying or dividing every part by the same number gives an equal ratio: the same mixture.

What to do: choose a recipe, then make the batch bigger or smaller. The bar splits the batch into equal parts. Then try writing the same recipe as × 2 or × 5.

What you are trying to notice: once you know one part, every ingredient follows. And when the recipe is written with more parts, does anything in the jug change?

🍎 Nutrition — Read the label (today's main activity)

Mode: practical and physical. Today the mathematics feeds something you can use every day: knowing what is in your food, cooking for more people, and sharing costs fairly.

No food is good or bad here. We are only finding out what is in it, using the same idea as the Recipe Bar.

What to do: food labels give amounts per 100 g. Choose a food and a portion size. The label finds the amount per 10 g for you; work out your portion, then reveal it to check.

What you are trying to notice: per 100 g lets you compare foods fairly, whatever the box size. Find one, then scale, to know what is in your bowl.

🛠️ Final Task — My recipe card

Your task: make a recipe card for a real or imagined recipe with at least three ingredients.

  • Write the recipe as a ratio in its simplest form, and show how you simplified it.
  • Scale it to a batch for a different number of people, finding one part (or one person) first.
  • Choose one ingredient's food label. Use its per 100 g values to work out what is in one portion.
  • Share the cost of the shopping between two households in a ratio, and show both shares.
  • stretch Explain why multiplying every part of a ratio by the same number makes the same mixture.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed recipe card, a short slideshow, or a spoken or video walkthrough.
