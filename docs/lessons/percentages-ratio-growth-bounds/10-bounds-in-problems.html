NEO Mathematics: Percentages, Ratio, Growth and Bounds

Bounds in problems

Driving Question: How sure can I be of this answer?

Lesson type: Core — using bounds to decide and to give an honest answer.

Stepping Stone: solve problems with bounds, including compound measures such as speed and density; use the bounds to make a decision, and give an answer to a suitable degree of accuracy: the most accurate one to which both bounds round to the same value.

You will need: calculating with bounds (Lesson 9) and rounding to significant figures, plus the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. A calculator is allowed.

🌱 Open Learner Guide 📘 Open Supporting Adult Guidance
🌿 Connection — Will the lorry fit?

Start here: a sign says a bridge is 4 m high, to the nearest metre. A lorry is 3.8 m tall, to 1 decimal place. Will the lorry fit under the bridge for sure?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

The bounds of an answer tell you how sure you can be. To make a decision, check whether every possible value gives the same answer. To give an answer to a suitable degree of accuracy, round the lower and upper bounds: if both round to the same value, you can give that value to that accuracy, and no more. For example, if 11.6 ≤ speed < 13.4, both round to 10 to 1 significant figure, but not to the same value to 2 significant figures: the speed is 10 m/s to 1 s.f.

🚶 Movement — Can you be sure?

What to do: answer each one quickly: yes, no or can't be sure. If it helps, thumbs up, thumbs down or a flat hand.

What you are trying to notice: picture the whole interval, not just the rounded value.

What to do: answer each question. Try it in the workshop if it helps.

What you are trying to notice: an answer can only be as accurate as its bounds agree.

🎨 Creativity — The How Sure? Workshop (today's main activity)

What to do: choose a problem and set each measurement and how it was rounded. The workshop finds the bounds of the answer. Read the how sure? ladder, then choose the accuracy to give the answer to. Then set the question below the ladder and see whether you can be sure. Then meet the briefs.

What you are trying to notice: measuring more precisely narrows the interval, and a narrower interval lets you decide, and give more accuracy.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Saying “I can't be sure yet” is an honest answer, not a failure. Take a slow breath.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: practical. Today the mathematics feeds honest answers.

What it feeds: today feeds knowing how much to trust a number. Buying enough paint, checking a parcel against a weight limit, or reading a speed from a map: the bounds tell you whether you can be sure, and how precisely you can say the answer, without claiming more than the measurements allow.

Carry one idea forward: how sure can I be?

🛠️ Final Task — How sure are you?

Your task: choose a real problem with two rounded measurements: a speed, a density, an area or an amount of paint or flooring.

  • State each measurement, its degree of accuracy and its bounds.
  • Find the upper and lower bounds of the answer, saying which bound of each value you used.
  • Give the answer to a suitable degree of accuracy, and explain why not more.
  • Make a decision (enough? under the limit? will it fit?) and say whether you can be sure.
  • stretch Say how precisely you would need to measure to be sure, and show it.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken or video walkthrough.
