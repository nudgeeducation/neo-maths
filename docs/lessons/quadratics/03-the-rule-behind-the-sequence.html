The rule behind the sequence

Driving Question: How can a sequence and an expression be the same quadratic?

Lesson type: Bridging — joining the two faces of a quadratic you have met so far: the growing sequence and the expression.

Stepping Stone: see that a quadratic sequence is generated by a quadratic expression (its rule); substitute the position n to build the sequence; and notice that the growing dot rectangle is that expression.

🌿 Connection — The dots are the expression

🔤 The idea for this lesson

In Lesson 1 you counted a growing rectangle of dots: 2, 6, 12, 20 — a quadratic sequence. In Lesson 2 you found its fingerprint, a constant second difference. But where does the sequence come from? Every sequence is made by a rule — and the rule that makes a quadratic sequence is a quadratic expression, the very thing you will expand next lesson.

Look at the rectangle of dots for stage n: it has n rows and n + 1 columns, so its count is n(n + 1). Split it into an n by n square and one extra column, and the count is n² + n. So the sequence, the rule and the expression are all the same thing: n(n + 1) = n² + n. The picture that made the sequence is the expression.

What to do: grow the rectangle stage by stage. Watch the green square and the blue extra column, and read how the count becomes n² + n.

What you are trying to notice: the count of dots, the area of the rectangle, and the expression n² + n are three names for the one quadratic. (When we study the general expression next, we will use x for the input instead of the position n.)

🪞 Reflection — Two ways to write the rule

What to do: a rule can be written as a product of brackets or as an expanded expression. Pick a value of n and substitute it into both forms — do they give the same term?

What you are trying to notice: (n + p)(n + q) and n² + (p + q)n + pq are the same rule — they always give the same sequence. Turning the brackets into the expanded form is called expanding, and it is exactly what we do next lesson.

🎨 Creativity — Why is the second difference 2a? stretch

What to do: this is the why behind Lesson 2, for when you are ready. Choose the number a in the rule y = a n², look at its first differences, and see why the second difference always comes out as 2a.

What you are trying to notice: the first differences themselves form a linear sequence — they go up by the same amount every time. That amount is the second difference, and it is 2a. So a quadratic's second difference is constant for exactly the reason a linear sequence's first difference is constant.

🧠 Why is the second difference 2a?

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, take the rule n² + n, write its terms, its first differences, and the differences of those. Explain in a sentence why the second difference is 2.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have joined two ideas that looked separate — a list of numbers and a symbol — into one quadratic. That kind of joining-up is what makes mathematics feel like one connected thing rather than many. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: did it change anything for you to see that the growing dots and the expression n² + n are the same? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. Rules and rectangles are honest mathematical tools, not the nourishment itself — so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the deep confidence that mathematics is connected — that a number sequence, a rule and a picture are not three topics to learn separately but one idea wearing three costumes. Every time you discover that two things you thought were different are secretly the same, mathematics gets smaller and more powerful at once: fewer things to remember, more ways to see. Learning to look for these hidden connections — "is this the same as something I already know?" — is one of the most freeing habits a mathematician can build.

Carry one idea forward: when you meet something new, ask what it is connected to — the links are where the understanding lives.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show that a quadratic sequence and a quadratic expression are the same thing.

  • Take the rule n(n + 1). Substitute n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 to generate the sequence, and show that the same rule can be written as n² + n.
  • Draw the growing dot rectangle for one stage and show how the count of dots equals the expression (a square plus one extra column).
  • Show that (n + 1)(n + 2) and n² + 3n + 2 give the same sequence by substituting a value into both.
  • stretch Explain why the second difference of a quadratic is always 2a, using the fact that the first differences form a linear sequence.
