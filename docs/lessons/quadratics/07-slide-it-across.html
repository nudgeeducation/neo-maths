Driving Question: Where does the curve's lowest point go, and why?

Lesson type: Core — the second lesson on moving and reshaping the parabola.

🌿 Connection — The same curve, moved sideways

🔤 The idea for this lesson

You have stretched the parabola; now you slide it. The rule y = (x − p)² looks almost like y = x², but with the x replaced by x − p. A table shows what that does: the heights are exactly the same as y = x² — the whole curve just moves sideways. Here is the surprise worth slowing down for: y = (x − 2)² slides to the right, not the left. Why? The lowest point happens when the bracket is 0, and x − 2 = 0 when x = 2 — so the bottom of the curve, which sat at x = 0, now sits at x = 2.

What to do: fill in y = (x − 2)² for each x (the y = x² row is given), then press Plot both.

What you are trying to notice: the two curves are identical in shape; the new one is just shifted 2 to the right. The lowest point moves from (0, 0) to (2, 0).

▦ Slide the table

Carry this forward: replacing x with x − p slides the curve p to the right. The shape never changes — only its position.

🚶 Movement — The p-slider

What to do: move the dial to change p and watch y = (x − p)² slide left and right. The faint dashed curve is y = x² (p = 0), kept still so you can compare. Watch the turning point and the line of symmetry travel with the curve.

What you are trying to notice: a positive p slides the curve to the right (subtracting moves it the "wrong" way at first glance); a negative p slides it left. The turning point sits at (p, 0) and the line of symmetry is x = p. The shape stays exactly the same.

🎛️ The p-slider

p = 0

Carry this forward: in y = (x − p)², the turning point is (p, 0) and the line of symmetry is x = p. Positive p → right; negative p → left.

🪞 Reflection — Find the bottom

What to do: for each equation, say where the turning point is by giving its x-coordinate, then check.

What you are trying to notice: the turning point of y = (x − p)² is at x = p — the value that makes the bracket 0. Watch the sign: (x + 1)² is really (x − (−1))², so its bottom is at x = −1.

🎨 Creativity — Write the equation stretch

What to do: you are given where a parabola's lowest point sits on the x-axis. Write the rule y = (x − p)² that puts it there by giving p.

What you are trying to notice: to place the bottom at x = 3 you need the bracket to be 0 there, so it must be (x − 3)² — you subtract the position. A bottom at x = −2 needs (x + 2)².

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have moved a whole curve across the plane by changing one number — and met the little surprise that subtracting slides it right. That is worth a quiet moment. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: did "minus moves it right" feel backwards at first? Many people find it does. What finally made it make sense for you — the table, or setting the bracket to zero? There is no right answer.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The sliding curve is a mathematical idea, not the nourishment itself — so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the confidence to trust structure over first impressions. "Subtract, so it moves right" feels backwards — but when you ask when is the bracket zero? the curve tells you the truth, and your intuition catches up. Learning to test a surprising result against the reason underneath it, rather than trusting a hunch, is one of the most valuable habits mathematics can give you.

Carry one idea forward: when a rule surprises you, find the reason underneath it — the reason is what you can rely on.
