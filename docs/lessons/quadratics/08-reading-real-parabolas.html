🌿 Connection — The shape is everywhere

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Every curve you have drawn this unit shows up in the real world. Throw a ball and its height over time traces a parabola. Water from a fountain, a hanging chain, the path of a jump — all parabolas. What makes them useful is the turning point: for an upside-down curve (a ∩) it is the highest point — a maximum; for a U it is the lowest point — a minimum. And it always means something real: the greatest height a ball reaches, and when; the price that earns the most money; the amount that costs the least to make.

What to do: look at the thrown-ball graph — its height in metres is drawn against time in seconds. Read the top of the arc.

What you are trying to notice: the top of the ∩ is the ball's greatest height, and the time beneath it is when that happens. The curve also shows when the ball leaves the ground and lands (where the height is 0).

🏀 A thrown ball

Carry this forward: a ∩ has a maximum turning point; a U has a minimum. The turning point always carries a real-world meaning.

🚶 Movement — Maximum or minimum?

What to do: for each real graph, decide whether its turning point is a maximum or a minimum, then check.

What you are trying to notice: a curve that opens downward (∩) has a maximum; one that opens upward (U) has a minimum. The context tells you which is the useful one — greatest height or most revenue (a maximum); least cost or smallest depth (a minimum).

🔀 Max or min?

Carry this forward: opens down → maximum; opens up → minimum.

🪞 Reflection — Read what it means

What to do: each graph models a real situation. Read its turning point from the labelled axes and type the two coordinates. When they are right, see what the point means.

What you are trying to notice: the two numbers of a turning point are not just coordinates — they are an answer. On a height–time graph, (3, 9) means "9 metres high, reached at 3 seconds". Reading a graph is translating a point back into the story it came from.

🎨 Creativity — The biggest growing space

What to do: you have 20 m of fencing to enclose a rectangular growing bed. If one side is w metres, the other is 10 − w (the four sides add to 20). So the area is A = w(10 − w) — a quadratic. Move the slider to change w and find the shape that gives the most growing area.

What you are trying to notice: the area graph is a ∩ — it has a maximum. The biggest area comes at the top of the curve, when the bed is a square.

🌱 Maximise the area

one side w = 3 m  ·  other side 10 − w = 7 m  ·  area = 21 m²

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, try 30 m of fencing (area = w(15 − w)). What side length gives the most area now — is it still a square?

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You began this unit with a row of growing numbers, and you end it reading the greatest height of a ball and the biggest garden you can grow. The same curve, all the way through. That is worth a quiet moment.

Notice one thing: across the whole unit — sequence, rule, expression, graph, real situation — which "face" of the quadratic felt most like yours? There is no right answer.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: contextual. Today one context genuinely lives in growing food, so the Cornerstone is honestly contextual — not a decoration on the mathematics.

What it feeds: the fencing problem is real. With a fixed length of fence, the shape you choose decides how much you can grow — and the quadratic shows that a square bed encloses the most area for the fence you have. So the same materials feed more plants, more food, less waste. That is mathematics doing an honest job for food-growing and sustainability: getting the most from what you already have. (The thrown ball and the fountain are real too, but they are settings for the mathematics rather than nourishment — so we name only the growing context as contextual.)

Carry one idea forward: when a resource is fixed, the mathematics can find the choice that gets the most from it.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for the unit

Your task: find or invent a real situation that makes a parabola, and explain what its turning point means.

  • Choose a context — a thrown or kicked ball, a jet of water, revenue against price, area against a side length, or one of your own.
  • Sketch its graph with labelled axes, mark the turning point, and say whether it is a maximum or a minimum.
  • Write one or two sentences explaining what the turning point means in the situation — the most or least of something, and when or where it happens.
  • stretch Use the fencing idea to show that, for a fixed perimeter, a square encloses the most area.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a hand-drawn labelled graph, a poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
