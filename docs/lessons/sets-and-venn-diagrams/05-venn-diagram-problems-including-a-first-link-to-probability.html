🌿 Connection — Chance from counts

Start here: three short probability questions. Answer with a fraction, a decimal or a percentage.

What you are trying to notice: a probability compares the event with everything that could happen: here, everyone in ℰ.

🎲 Chance from counts

🔤 The idea for this lesson

When one region of a Venn diagram is unknown, call it x. Use the clues to write every other region in terms of x, such as 23 − x. All the regions together make ℰ, so their total gives an equation to solve.

For an element chosen at random, P(event) = number in the eventn(ℰ).

Today's main activity, the Survey Solver, is in Nutrition.

🚶 Movement — The Probability Picker

What to do: 30 learners were asked whether they play guitar (G) or piano (P). Tap regions to build an event, and watch its probability. Then answer the four questions.

What you are trying to notice: the numerator is the total of the lit regions; the denominator is always n(ℰ) = 30.

🪞 Reflection — Which expression goes where?

What to do: choose the expression for each region from the clues. Then choose the right equation.

What you are trying to notice: a clue such as n(A) = 15 counts the whole circle, so the region “only A” is 15 − x, not 15.

🍎 Nutrition — The Survey Solver (today's main activity)

Mode: practical. Surveys are how schools, councils and companies find out what people do. Their results often come as overlapping counts, with one number missing.

What to do: for each survey, type an expression for every region (using x where you need it) and check. Then solve the equation from the total, and read a probability from the finished diagram.

What you are trying to notice: once every region is written in terms of x, the total turns the whole survey into one equation.

📋 The Survey Solver

Tap regions, or use the buttons, to build an event:

What it feeds: reading survey results exactly. A headline such as “23 use S and 19 use T” does not mean 42 people: some are counted twice. Asking “who is in both?” is how you avoid being misled by overlapping counts.

Carry one idea forward: name the unknown, then let the total find it.

🛠️ Final Task — My survey problem

Your task: write a survey problem about two or three sets (for example clubs, music or travel), with one region unknown.

  • Give clues that fix the diagram, and call the unknown region x.
  • Solve your problem: write each region in terms of x, form the equation from the total, and find x.
  • Give two probabilities from your finished diagram, as fractions in their simplest form.
  • stretch Use a three-set diagram, with x in the centre.
