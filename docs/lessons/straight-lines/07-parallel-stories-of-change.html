NEO Mathematics: Straight Lines

Parallel stories of change

Driving Question: Can two lines change alike but begin differently?

Lesson type: Relating — comparing lines that share a gradient but start in different places.

Stepping Stone: recognise and build parallel lines — lines are parallel when they have the same gradient m and different intercepts c.

NEO Cornerstones: Connection · Movement · Reflection · Creativity · Rest · Nutrition

🌿 Connection — Parallel Line Challenge

🔤 New words for this lesson

Parallel — two lines that run in the same direction and never meet, staying the same distance apart.
Same gradient — parallel lines have equal m: they are exactly as steep as each other.
Different intercept — they cross the y-axis at different c, so one sits above the other.

What to do: for each line shown, decide which equation from the list is parallel to it. Press Check, and Next for a new challenge.

What you are trying to notice: parallel is all about the gradient. Two lines are parallel when their m values match — the intercept c only decides where each one sits, not whether they are parallel.

🛤️ Parallel Line Challenge

Carry this forward: lines are parallel when they share the same gradient m. Different intercepts just stack them apart — they still never meet.

🚶 Movement — Make it parallel

What to do: a solid reference line is drawn. Move the m and c sliders to make your line parallel to it. It only turns green when your gradient matches and your line sits somewhere different (a real parallel, not the same line on top).

What you are trying to notice: matching the gradient is what makes lines parallel — wherever you then put the intercept, they stay parallel. Match c as well and it stops being a separate line.

📐 Make it Parallel

Carry this forward: to make a line parallel to another, give it the same gradient and a different intercept. Same m, new c.

🪞 Reflection — Parallel or not?

What to do: each card gives two equations. Decide from the numbers alone whether the two lines are parallel or not, then check against the graph.

What you are trying to notice: only the gradient decides. Same m means parallel, whatever the intercepts. The same intercept does not make lines parallel — lines that cross the y-axis at the same place but have different gradients still spread apart.

🔎 Parallel or not?

Carry this forward: to tell if two lines are parallel, compare only their gradients. Equal m → parallel. Different m → not parallel, whatever the intercepts.

🎨 Creativity — Two plans, side by side

What to do: each situation offers two plans that charge the same rate but have different fixed fees. Write the equation for each, and see them come out parallel. Then answer: which is always cheaper?

What you are trying to notice: the same rate gives the same gradient, so the two lines are parallel and never meet — the cheaper plan stays cheaper forever. Parallel lines never cross, so the two costs are never equal.

💷 Two Plans, Side by Side

And which plan is always cheaper?

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, invent two plans with the same rate but different fees, write both equations, and show they are parallel.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Seeing that two lines can change at exactly the same rate yet begin in different places — running side by side forever — is a quietly satisfying idea. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: when two lines were parallel, what told you they would never meet — the picture, or the matching gradients? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. When two deals charge the same rate, their lines run parallel.

Why it matters: two phone plans that both charge £3 a gigabyte but have different monthly fees; two gyms with the same per-session price but different joining fees; two savings accounts paying in the same amount each week from different starting balances — in each pair the lines are parallel, because the rate (the gradient) is the same. When lines are parallel they never meet, which means one option is cheaper than the other forever — the gap never closes. Spotting parallel deals tells you instantly that there is no "break-even" point to hunt for: the fixed difference simply stays. When the rates differ, though, the lines do cross — and that crossing point is exactly what the next lesson is about.

Next time two deals seem to charge the same per unit, picture two parallel lines — the cheaper one wins every time.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: work with parallel lines.

  • Take any line and write the equation of a line that is parallel to it. Explain how you know they are parallel, and draw both to check they never meet.
  • Someone says two lines are parallel because they cross the y-axis at the same point. Explain why that is not enough, using a quick example.
  • Describe a real pair of plans that charge the same rate but different fixed fees. Write both equations, show they are parallel, and say which is always cheaper and why.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written solution, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
