NEO Mathematics: Straight Lines

Conversion graphs as models

Driving Question: How can a line translate one quantity into another?

Lesson type: Modelling — using a straight line to convert between two quantities, reading it both ways.

Stepping Stone: read a conversion graph in both directions, and interpret the gradient as the conversion rate.

🌿 Connection — Conversion Graph Translator

🔤 New words for this lesson

Conversion graph — a straight line that translates one quantity into another (miles into km, £ into €).
Reading both ways — go up from one axis to the line, then across to the other; it works in either direction.
Conversion rate — the gradient of the line: how many of one unit you get for each of the other.

What to do: use the conversion line to answer the question. Go up from the value you are given until you hit the line, then across to read the answer off the other axis. Type it in, press Reveal, and Next for another.

What you are trying to notice: the same line converts both ways — up-and-across, or across-and-down. One line does every conversion between the two quantities.

🔁 Conversion Graph Translator

Carry this forward: a conversion graph works both ways. Go up-and-across to convert one way, across-and-down to convert back — the single line does both.

🚶 Movement — Two-way reader

What to do: pick a conversion, then drag the point along the line. Dashed guides drop to both axes, showing the matching pair — for example "5 miles = 8 km". Drag it anywhere to convert any value.

What you are trying to notice: a single point links a value on one axis to a value on the other, so one line reads both directions at once. The steeper the line, the bigger the conversion rate.

↔️ Two-way Reader

Carry this forward: to convert with a graph, go up-and-across for one direction and across-and-down for the other. The same line, and the same point, does both.

🪞 Reflection — Read it right?

What to do: each card shows a conversion and how someone read it. Decide whether their reading is right or a slip, then check against the graph.

What you are trying to notice: the common slips are reading off the wrong axis, or not using the line (just multiplying without checking the graph — which goes wrong when the line does not start at zero, like °C to °F).

🔎 Read it right?

Carry this forward: always read to the line and check which axis is which. When a conversion line does not pass through the origin, you cannot just multiply.

🎨 Creativity — Holiday money

What to do: you are on holiday with a pounds-to-euros conversion graph. Use it to answer each real question — reading up-and-across, or across-and-down, depending which way you are converting. Type your answer and check.

What you are trying to notice: the same graph answers "how many euros do I get?" and "how much is that in pounds?" — one line, both directions. That is what makes a conversion graph so useful.

🏖️ Holiday Money

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, draw a conversion graph for a currency or unit you care about, and use it to answer a question of your own — both ways.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Seeing that one straight line can translate endlessly between two quantities — both ways, forever — is a quietly elegant idea. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: when you read the graph, did you find one direction easier than the other — up-and-across, or across-and-down? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. Conversion graphs turn a rate into an instant look-up you can read both ways.

Why it matters: changing money on holiday (pounds to euros and back), reading a recipe from another country (cups to grams, °F to °C for the oven), planning a drive abroad (miles to kilometres, and speed limits in km/h), or checking a weather forecast in the "wrong" units — all of these are a single straight line you can read in either direction. The gradient is the conversion rate: a steeper line means more of one unit per unit of the other. Some conversions pass through the origin (0 miles is 0 km) and some do not — 0°C is 32°F, not 0 — which is exactly why you read to the line rather than just multiplying. Once the graph is drawn, every conversion is a quick up-and-across or across-and-down.

Next time you need the "other units", picture the conversion line — one look-up, both directions.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: build and use a conversion graph.

  • Draw a conversion graph from a rate you know (for example 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km, or £1 = €1.20). Plot a couple of points and join them with a straight line.
  • Use your graph to convert both ways: pick a value on each axis and read across to the other. Show the up-and-across (and across-and-down) on the graph.
  • Explain what the gradient of your line means as a conversion rate, and — if you use °C to °F — explain why you must read to the line and cannot simply multiply.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written solution, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
