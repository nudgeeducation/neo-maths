## Unit Driving Questions
- **Angles Tell Us Why:** Can a diagram prove more than it first shows?
- **Perimeter and Area: Same Shape Story?** Can two shapes use the same boundary but hold different space?

## The mathematical story
**Angles.** An angle is an amount of **turn**, measured in **degrees**: a full turn is 360°, so half a turn is 180° and a quarter turn, a right angle, is 90°. Measuring comes first, with an estimate before every reading so the protractor's two scales never trick anyone. From the half turn comes the first piece of reasoning: angles on a **straight line** add to 180°, angles at a **point** add to 360°, and **vertically opposite** angles are equal, *because* each makes a straight line with the same neighbour. Tearing the corners from any triangle and laying them side by side makes a straight line, so the angles of a **triangle** add to 180°; isosceles, equilateral and right-angled triangles then each have angle facts of their own. Finally, a diagonal splits any **quadrilateral** into two triangles, so its angles add to 360°, and the properties of special quadrilaterals let a learner find missing angles in a chain of reasons: *because … therefore …*. Measuring suggests; a reason proves.

**Area and perimeter.** **Perimeter** is the distance around a shape; **area** is the space inside it. The same fence can enclose very different fields, so the two are not the same story. Area is counted in squares (cm², m²), which leads to length × width for a **rectangle**. A **triangle** is half of a rectangle drawn around it, so its area is half of base × **perpendicular height**, for any triangle, not just right-angled ones. Sliding a triangle from one end of a **parallelogram** to the other turns it into a rectangle; two copies of a **trapezium** make a parallelogram. **Composite shapes** (an L-shaped room) are split into rectangles and triangles, in more than one way, always giving the same area. Throughout, lengths come in metres and centimetres, and every answer carries its units.

**Arc (Angles):** measuring turns → straight lines and points → triangles → because–therefore with quadrilaterals.
**Arc (Area):** perimeter or area? → rectangles and triangles → parallelograms and trapezia → composite shapes and design choices.

## Stepping Stones
**Angles**
- **Estimate, measure and draw** angles to the nearest degree, and classify them as acute, right, obtuse or reflex.
- Use **angles on a straight line** (180°), **at a point** (360°) and **vertically opposite angles**, giving the reason for each step.
- **Derive and use** the angle sum of a triangle, and the angle facts of isosceles, equilateral and right-angled triangles.
- Use the angle sum and **properties of quadrilaterals** to find missing angles in a chain of reasons (*because … therefore …*).

**Area and perimeter**
- **Distinguish perimeter from area**, and show that shapes with the same perimeter can have different areas (and the reverse).
- Find the **area of rectangles and triangles**, explaining why a triangle's area is half of base × perpendicular height.
- Find the **area of parallelograms and trapezia** by rearranging them into shapes already known.
- Find the **perimeter and area of composite shapes**, in metres and centimetres, and use them to make and justify **design choices**.

## Notes for building
- **Pictures first:** every angle fact and area formula is seen in a diagram before it is stated, then given a reason. Where a rule is spotted, add a short "Why must it be?" stretch (GreenPrint: justify, not just pattern).
- **Diagrams:** draw them accurately when learners measure, and mark them "not drawn accurately" when they must reason instead. Label angles with three letters or a small letter, and mark right angles with a square.
- **Measuring:** always estimate first ("Is it more or less than a right angle?"), so the protractor's inner and outer scales never mislead.
- **Reasons:** accept reasons in learners' own words, and model the standard phrasing: *angles on a straight line add to 180°*, *vertically opposite angles are equal*, *angles in a triangle add to 180°*, *base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal*, *angles in a quadrilateral add to 360°*.
- **Perpendicular height:** show it outside the triangle for obtuse triangles, and never use the slanted side as the height.
- **Units:** every length carries cm or m, and every area cm² or m². Answers are accepted with or without units, and in either unit of a pair (QA gate 6a), building on Place Value Lesson 8.
- **Vocabulary** (GreenPrint v2.0): *turn, degree, protractor, acute, right, obtuse, reflex, vertex, straight line, vertically opposite, perpendicular, parallel, isosceles, equilateral, scalene, quadrilateral, parallelogram, rhombus, trapezium, kite, perimeter, area, square centimetre, base, perpendicular height, composite*.
- **Contexts** should be calm and age-respectful: rooms, gardens, fences, tiles, paths, sails, ramps, maps and design.
- **Age and tone:** this may be a learner's first geometry at NEO after an interrupted primary education. Keep reading load light, and never imply that this "should already be known". Learner-facing text says Foundations, never Year 7.
- **Calculators** are not needed: lengths are chosen to keep the arithmetic friendly.
- **Answer checking** (QA gate 6a): accept number words, fractions such as 1/2 where an answer is non-integer, the degree sign or none, and units or none.
- **Symbols palette** on in-lesson answer boxes that need °, ², × or ÷.
- **Rubric labels:** Approaching Proficiency · Developing Proficiency · Proficiency · Refining Proficiency.
- **Reuse:** the **Geometry Studio** (Measure → Angle and Measure → Distance) offers an optional "measure to check" task in Angles Lesson 4. The Year 9 *2D and 3D Shapes* lessons are the onward Reconnection Routes, not material for this unit.
- **Site:** two new units under the Foundations Year 7 heading, `angles` and `perimeter-area`, sharing one diagnostic (`studio`). Classroom gets a topic when the first lesson goes live.
