## Unit Driving Question
How does a formula capture a relationship, and how do I turn it round to find what I need?

## The mathematical story
A **formula** is a sentence about a relationship, written in letters.
- *A = lw* says how the area of a rectangle depends on its length and width.
- *C = 3 + 2m* says how a taxi fare depends on the miles travelled.

Learners have used letters before, mostly as **unknowns**, where one hidden number makes an equation true. In a formula, the letters are **variables**: they can take many values, and the formula says how they move together.

The unit begins with **meaning**. It asks what a letter stands for, and how *equation*, *formula*, *expression* and *identity* differ. A good test: an equation is true for particular values, an identity for every value, and a formula links quantities that vary. Along the way, learners meet the **conventions**: 3*n*, *ab*, *n*², *n*/2.

It then turns to **using** formulae.
- **Substituting**, including **negative** and **fractional** values, where brackets and the order of operations matter: (−3)² is not −3².
- **Writing** formulae from words and diagrams.
- **Deriving** a formula from a pattern or a shape, by noticing what stays the same and what changes.

Next comes **changing the subject**. This is the balance from Inequalities Lesson 2 again, with letters on both sides: undo the operations in reverse order, doing the same to both sides. The question "what is the length if the area is 36 and the width is 4?" becomes the formula *l* = *A* / *w*.

Finally, learners **use and check** formulae in context. They decide whether to substitute or rearrange first, attend to **units**, and check that an answer makes sense by substituting it back.

**Arc:** what a letter means → substitute (including awkward values) → write a formula from words and pictures → derive a formula yourself → turn a formula round → use and check it in the real world.

## Stepping Stones
- Distinguish a letter as an **unknown** from a letter as a **variable**.
- Recognise and use **expression**, **equation**, **formula** and **identity**.
- Read and write algebra using the standard **notational conventions**.
- **Substitute** positive, negative and fractional values into expressions and formulae, using brackets and the order of operations correctly.
- **Write** a formula from a description in words or a diagram, and use it.
- **Derive** a formula from a pattern or a shape, and explain why it works.
- **Change the subject** of a formula in which the new subject appears once, using inverse operations and keeping the balance.
- **Use and check** formulae in context, including units, and judge whether an answer is reasonable.

## Notes for building
- **Contexts** should be calm and age-respectful: taxi and phone tariffs, recipes and scaling, temperature conversion (°C and °F), speed, distance and time, perimeter, area and volume, hourly pay, and the cost of a gig or a day out.
- **Vocabulary** (GreenPrint v2.0): use *expression, equation, formula, identity, variable, unknown, term, coefficient, subject, substitute* precisely from Lesson 1. Never use "letter maths" or "the answer letter".
- **Negatives and fractions**: substitution with negatives is the most common error. Lesson 2 makes **brackets visible**, writing (−3)² rather than −3², so learners *see* why a negative squared is positive.
- **Rearranging is the balance again.** Lesson 5 reuses the balance from Inequalities Lesson 2, now level rather than tipped, so rearranging feels familiar rather than new. The inverse function machine (×3 then +2, undone by −2 then ÷3) gives a second view of the same step.
- **Answer checking for algebra.** Rearranged formulae have many correct forms: *t* = (*v* − *u*)/*a*, *t* = (*v* − *u*) ÷ *a*, (*v* − *u*)/*a* = *t*. Check an answer by **evaluating it at test values** of the other variables, never by matching strings. This extends QA gate 6a to algebraic answers; the checker will be built once and reused across the unit.
- **Graphs** in any resource follow GreenPrint v2.3 (numbered, labelled axes).
- **Year 10 distinction:** Foundations keeps the subject appearing once. Squares and square roots appear only in the simplest case (*A* = *s*², so *s* = √*A*), as a stretch.
