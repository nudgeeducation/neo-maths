## Unit Driving Question
How do quantities scale and grow, and how sure can we be of the answer?

## The mathematical story
One idea runs through the whole unit: **multiplying by a number** describes a change. A ratio scales a recipe; a constant *k* links two quantities in proportion; a multiplier such as 1.15 or 0.8 is a percentage change; repeating the multiplier is growth; and the size of a rounding error decides how far an answer can drift.

It begins with **ratio**: a ratio compares parts, so 2 : 3 means 2 parts to 3 parts, 5 parts in all. Sharing £40 in the ratio 2 : 3 finds one part first (the **unitary method**), and "find one, then scale" becomes the unit's first habit.

**Proportion** follows directly, next to ratio: when one quantity doubles and the other doubles too, they are in **direct proportion** (*y* = *kx*, a straight line through the origin); when one doubles and the other halves, they are in **inverse proportion** (*y* = *k*/*x*, a curve that never meets the axes). Finding *k* from one pair of values turns any proportion question into substitution. Squares, cubes and roots extend the same idea (*y* = *kx*², *y* = *k*/√*x*).

Then **percentages become multipliers**. 15% of a number is 0.15 × it; increasing by 15% is × 1.15; decreasing by 20% is × 0.8. With one multiplier, every percentage question has the same shape. **Percentage change** compares the change with the original value. **Reverse percentages** divide by the multiplier: if a price after a 20% rise is £96, the original is 96 ÷ 1.2 = £80, not 96 less 20% of 96.

**Repeated change** multiplies again and again: £500 at 4% compound interest for 3 years is 500 × 1.04³, not 500 + 3 × 20. Depreciation is the same with a multiplier below 1. **Growth models** step back and compare **adding the same amount** (linear growth) with **multiplying by the same amount** (exponential growth), in tables and graphs, and ask which model fits a situation.

The unit ends by asking **how sure** an answer is. A length given as 7.3 cm to 1 decimal place could be anything from 7.25 cm up to (not including) 7.35 cm: those are its **lower and upper bounds**. **Calculating with bounds** asks which bounds to combine: the largest area uses both upper bounds, but the largest result of a division uses the upper bound of the numerator and the lower bound of the denominator. **Bounds in problems** uses them to decide an answer to a suitable degree of accuracy, the question every measurement-based answer should ask.

**Arc:** compare and share → in proportion, find *k* → percentage as a multiplier → change, compared with the original → undo a change → repeat a change → add or multiply? → what could it have been before rounding? → which bounds give the extremes? → how sure is the answer?

## Stepping Stones
- Write and simplify **ratios**, divide a quantity in a given ratio, and use the **unitary method**.
- Recognise **direct and inverse proportion**, set them up as *y* = *kx* and *y* = *k*/*x* (including squares, cubes and roots), find *k*, and link each to its graph.
- Use **percentages as multipliers**, including finding one number as a percentage of another.
- Find a **percentage increase or decrease**, and express a change as a percentage of the original.
- Solve **reverse percentage** problems by dividing by the multiplier.
- Solve **repeated percentage change** problems, including **compound interest and depreciation**.
- Compare **linear and exponential growth** in tables, graphs and contexts, and choose a model.
- Find the **upper and lower bounds** of a value rounded to a given degree of accuracy.
- **Calculate with bounds**, choosing which bounds give the largest and smallest results.
- Use bounds to **solve problems** and to give an answer to a suitable degree of accuracy.

## Notes for building
- **Contexts** should be calm and age-respectful: recipes, shared costs and bills, phone and data plans, sale prices and VAT, savings and loans, a car or phone losing value, populations and plant growth, travel times and speeds, and measurements made with a ruler or scales. Avoid gambling and debt as entertainment.
- **Vocabulary** (GreenPrint v2.0): use *ratio, part, unitary method, proportion, directly proportional, inversely proportional, constant of proportionality, multiplier, percentage change, original value, reverse percentage, compound interest, depreciation, linear, exponential, upper bound, lower bound, degree of accuracy* precisely. Say *numerator* and *denominator* throughout. Use ∝ once, with "is proportional to".
- **Exact values on screen** (Gerry, Angles L3): sliders and inputs snap so that every value shown is exact. Money is shown to the penny. When a rounded value is shown, say so explicitly ("to the nearest penny"); never show ≈ for a value the lesson treats as exact.
- **Bounds:** the upper bound is not reached by the rounded value's interval (7.25 ≤ *x* < 7.35); say "up to but not including" and accept 7.35 as the upper bound, as the specification does. Do not write 7.3499….
- **Proof** (the "pattern, then proof" principle): rules spotted from examples get a short "Why must it be?" stretch with a labelled diagram and per-step highlights (Gerry, Angles L2). Candidates: why a 10% rise then a 10% fall is not back to the start (1.1 × 0.9 = 0.99); why dividing by the multiplier undoes a change; why inverse proportion's graph never meets the axes.
- **Calculators** are allowed from Lesson 3 onwards, as in the iGCSE calculator paper; the lessons still ask for a mental estimate first. Lessons 1 and 2 use friendly numbers.
- **Answer checking** (QA gate 6a) must accept every equivalent form: fractions, decimals, percentages with or without %, money with or without £ and pence, ratios with spaces or none (2:3, 2 : 3) and in any equivalent simplest form where the task allows, and number words.
- **Symbols palette** on every in-lesson answer box that needs symbols (%, £, :, ∝, ×, ÷, ², √, fraction), using the `neo-mathfield` class.
- **Rubric labels:** Approaching Proficiency · Developing Proficiency · Proficiency · Refining Proficiency.
- **Graphs** in any resource follow GreenPrint v2.3 (numbered, labelled axes, italic *x* and *y*, O at the origin).
- **Field kit:** add the new unit to `FIELDKIT_UNITS` and to `scripts/fieldkits/make_fieldkits.js` (from the expedition) when the first lesson is built.
- **Year 9 distinction:** no lesson repeats a Year 9 objective as its main aim. Lesson 8 begins from Year 9's informal "what could it have been?" and makes bounds exact and usable.
