## Unit Driving Question
How do sets organise information and help us count?

## The mathematical story
One idea runs through the whole unit: **a set is a collection defined by a rule, and a Venn diagram is a picture of every region the rules make.**

It begins with **belonging**. A set is a well-defined collection: for any object, we can say whether it is in the set or not. Sets are written in curly brackets, {2, 4, 6, 8}, or by a rule, {even numbers less than 10}. An object in the set is an **element** (6 ∈ *A*; 5 ∉ *A*), and *n*(*A*) counts the elements. The **universal set**, ℰ, holds everything under discussion; the **empty set**, ∅, holds nothing.

Two sets make four regions. **Intersection**, *A* ∩ *B*, is what is in both (“and”); **union**, *A* ∪ *B*, is what is in at least one (“or”, including both); the **complement**, *A*′, is everything in ℰ that is not in *A* (“not”). A Venn diagram makes these visible: each region of the picture is a combination of “in” and “not in”, and shading shows any combination, such as (*A* ∪ *B*)′ or *A* ∩ *B*′.

Then sets can **hide inside one another or be empty**. If every element of *A* is in *B*, *A* is a **subset** of *B* (*A* ⊂ *B*), and its circle sits inside *B*'s. Sets can also be defined **algebraically**, {*x* : *x* is an integer, 2 < *x* ≤ 6} = {3, 4, 5, 6}, which links set notation to inequalities.

A **third set** makes eight regions. Placing elements in a three-set Venn diagram is systematic: work from the centre (in all three) outwards. Counting with *n*(…) then answers questions such as “how many are in exactly two sets?”.

Finally, Venn diagrams **solve problems**. Given totals, fill the diagram from the middle outwards, using an algebraic *x* when a region is unknown, and form an equation from the total. A Venn diagram of counts also gives **probabilities**: P(*A* ∩ *B*) is the number in *A* ∩ *B* over the number in ℰ, written as a stacked fraction.

**Arc:** what belongs? → and, or, not → inside another, or empty → three sets, eight regions → count, solve and find a chance.

## Stepping Stones
- Understand a **set** as a well-defined collection; list its **elements**; use { }, ∈, ∉, *n*(*A*), ℰ and ∅.
- Find and shade the **intersection**, **union** and **complement** of sets (*A* ∩ *B*, *A* ∪ *B*, *A*′), including combinations such as (*A* ∪ *B*)′ and *A* ∩ *B*′.
- Understand **subsets** (*A* ⊂ *B*) and the empty set, and list sets **defined algebraically**, such as {*x* : *x* is an integer, −2 ≤ *x* < 3}.
- Represent three sets on a **three-set Venn diagram** and use *n*(…) to count its regions.
- Solve **Venn diagram problems**, including with an unknown *x*, and find **probabilities** from a Venn diagram.

## Notes for building
- **Notation** follows the specification exactly: ℰ for the universal set, ∅ for the empty set, *A*′ for the complement, ∪, ∩, ∈, ∉, ⊂, *n*(*A*), and set-builder notation with a colon, {*x* : …}. Read each symbol aloud on first use (“*A* intersect *B*: in *A* and in *B*”) and keep the words beside the symbol for the first two lessons.
- **Venn diagrams:** ℰ is always a rectangle containing the circles; label every circle and the rectangle. A region's count is written inside it; a list of elements is written in it. Elements outside every circle but inside ℰ are part of the diagram. Shaded regions use one calm colour with a clear outline, and every shading is also described in words for screen readers.
- **Contexts** should be calm and age-respectful: number sets (factors, multiples, primes, squares), shared interests and clubs, transport to college, music and sport choices, languages spoken, pets, and features of shapes. Avoid sorting people by appearance, ability or background.
- **Vocabulary** (GreenPrint v2.0): use *set, element, member, universal set, empty set, intersection, union, complement, subset, region, Venn diagram* precisely. Say “intersection” and “union”, not “overlap” and “both together”, once the symbols are introduced (the informal word may appear once as a gloss).
- **Exact values on screen:** counts are whole numbers; probabilities are exact fractions, written as stacked fractions (GreenPrint v2.6) and accepted in any equivalent form.
- **Proof** (the “pattern, then proof” principle): rules spotted from examples get a short “Why must it be?” stretch with a labelled diagram and per-step highlights. Candidates: why *n*(*A* ∪ *B*) = *n*(*A*) + *n*(*B*) − *n*(*A* ∩ *B*) (the intersection is counted twice); why (*A* ∪ *B*)′ = *A*′ ∩ *B*′ (De Morgan, as an optional stretch only).
- **Answer checking** (QA gate 6a): accept a set's elements in any order, with or without spaces or brackets, and repeated elements ignored; accept ∈ / “is in”, ∅ / { } / “empty”; accept equivalent notation where it is genuinely equivalent (for example *A* ∩ *B* and *B* ∩ *A*); fractions, decimals and percentages for probabilities; number words.
- **Symbols palette** on every in-lesson answer box that needs symbols (∈, ∉, ∪, ∩, ′, ℰ, ∅, ⊂, { }, :, ≤, <), using the `neo-mathfield` class. The palette must insert these exact Unicode characters.
- **Rubric labels:** Approaching Proficiency · Developing Proficiency · Proficiency · Refining Proficiency.
- **Field kit:** add the new unit to `FIELDKIT_UNITS` and to `scripts/fieldkits/make_fieldkits.js` (from the expedition) when the first lesson is built.
- **Year 9 distinction:** Lessons 1 and 2 teach Foundation notation that this cohort has not met; Lessons 3 to 5 carry the Higher tier aims. When Year 9 Unit 10 is built, it reuses these interactives at Foundation depth, and Year 10's first two lessons become Reconnection Routes.
