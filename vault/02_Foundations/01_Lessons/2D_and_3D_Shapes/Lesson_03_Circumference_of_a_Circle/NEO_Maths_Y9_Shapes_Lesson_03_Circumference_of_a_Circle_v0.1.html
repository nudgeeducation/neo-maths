Circumference of a Circle

Driving Question: How far is it around a circle — and how does that distance depend on the circle's width?

Lesson type: Relationship investigation — discovering that the distance around a circle is a fixed multiple of the distance across it.

Stepping Stone: work with circles — find the circumference of a circle using C = π d (equivalently C = 2 π r), and understand π as the constant ratio of circumference to diameter.

NEO Cornerstones: Connection · Movement · Reflection · Creativity · Rest · Nutrition

🌿 Connection — How does the distance around depend on the width?

🔤 New words: the parts of a circle

Before you begin, here are the words we will use:

Circumference (C) — the distance all the way around the circle (its perimeter).
Diameter (d) — the distance straight across the circle, through the centre.
Radius (r) — the distance from the centre to the edge. The diameter is two radii: d = 2r.
Centre — the middle point, the same distance from every point on the circle.

This lesson is about the circumference — and how it depends on the diameter.

What to do: change the diameter of the circle, then plot the point G = (d, C) onto the graph. Plot several, watch where they land, and try to work out the gradient of the line and its equation before you reveal them.

What you are trying to notice: the points all lie on a straight line through the origin — so the circumference is always the same number of times the diameter. That number is π.

Discover it yourself.

1. The gradient. Divide any circumference by its diameter. What number do you get each time?

2. The equation. Write a formula for the circumference C in terms of the diameter d.

Carry this forward: because C = π d, the circumference grows in direct proportion to the diameter — double the width, double the distance around. Next, in Movement, you will see why that multiplier is π.

🚶 Movement — Why is the multiplier π?

What to do: roll the wheel one full turn along the track. The distance it covers is the circumference. Compare that distance with the diameters marked on the track.

What you are trying to notice: one full roll always covers a little more than three diameters — about 3.14 of them. That is π.

Carry this forward: unrolling the wheel turns the curved circumference into a straight length — about π diameters. That is exactly the multiplier the graph showed: C = π d.

🪞 Reflection — True or false, and how do you know?

What to do: decide whether each statement is true or false, then reveal the reasoning. You are being asked to justify, not just answer.

What you are trying to notice: two secure facts — C = π d, and π is a fixed ratio — let you judge any claim about circumference.

⚖️ Circle Reasoner

Carry this forward: saying why a circumference claim holds — because C ÷ d is always π — is what makes the rule trustworthy for a circle you have never measured.

🎨 Creativity — Can you estimate the distance around real things?

What to do: pick a round object, read its diameter, and predict its circumference before you check. A quick estimate is "a bit more than three diameters".

What you are trying to notice: the same rule, C ≈ 3.14 × d, works for a coin, a wheel and the Moon alike.

🌍 Estimate then check

A £1 coin — diameter 2.3 cm.

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, make a "π tape" — mark a strip in diameters and show how the circumference stretches to about 3.14 of them. Then estimate a round object near you.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let the ratio settle

You have found a number hiding in every circle — the same ratio, however large or small the circle is. That is a quiet, remarkable thing.

Notice one thing: which showed it more clearly for you — the points lining up on the graph, or the wheel rolling out about three-and-a-bit diameters? There is no right answer; just the one that made π feel real to you.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. This lesson reaches something you can hold: the label on a tin of food. To wrap a paper label right around a tin, the label has to be as long as the tin's circumference.

Why it matters: a food tin 7 cm across needs a label about π × 7 ≈ 22 cm long to go once around — plus a little to overlap and glue. Get it wrong and the label gapes or won't meet. Every can on a supermarket shelf has been sized with C = π d.

Where else does "the distance around" decide a real length — a bicycle tyre, a running track, a ring, a roll of tape?

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: find some circumferences, and explain the rule that makes it possible.

  • For three circles of your choice, record: the diameter, the circumference (using C = π d, with π ≈ 3.14), and the ratio C ÷ d.
  • Complete this sentence in your own words: "The circumference of a circle is always about ___ times its diameter, because…"
  • Explain what π is, and why the points (d, C) make a straight line through the origin.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a short written table, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough with a labelled sketch.
