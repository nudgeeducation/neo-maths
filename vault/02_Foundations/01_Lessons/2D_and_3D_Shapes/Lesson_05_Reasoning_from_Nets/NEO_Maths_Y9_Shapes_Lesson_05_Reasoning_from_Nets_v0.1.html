NEO Mathematics: 2D and 3D Shapes

Reasoning from Nets

Driving Question: How does a flat shape fold up into a solid — and which flat shapes will?

Lesson type: Spatial reasoning — moving between a flat net and the 3D solid it folds into.

Stepping Stone: reason from nets — recognise that a flat net folds into a solid, that each 2D face of the net becomes a face of the 3D shape, and identify and construct the nets of common solids.

NEO Cornerstones: Connection · Movement · Reflection · Creativity · Rest · Nutrition

🌿 Connection — Which face lands where?

🔤 New words for this lesson

Net — a flat shape that folds up to make a solid, with every face laid out and joined along edges.
Face — one flat surface of the solid; each face of the net becomes a face of the solid.
Edge — a line where two faces meet; folding happens along edges.
Opposite faces — two faces that end up on opposite sides of the solid, never touching.

What to do: look at the net below. Predict which solid it folds into, and which numbered face will end up opposite the shaded one. Then fold it in Movement to check.

What you are trying to notice: every face of the net has a fixed destination on the solid — the fold is not a mystery, it is a mapping you can reason out.

Carry this forward: a net records every face of a solid and exactly how they join. That is why, in the next lesson, a net is the key to a solid's surface area — it lays every face out flat to measure.

🚶 Movement — Fold the net into a solid

What to do: choose a solid, then drag the fold slider from flat to folded and watch the net rise into shape. Drag on the shape itself to rotate it and see the back.

What you are trying to notice: each flat face swings up along an edge and meets its neighbours — the flat net and the solid are the same faces, just arranged in 2D or in 3D.

Carry this forward: because folding just swings each face along an edge, the faces never change size — so the area of the net and the surface area of the solid are exactly the same. Hold onto that for Lesson 6.

🪞 Reflection — Will it close into a cube?

What to do: each arrangement below is six squares joined edge to edge. Decide whether it will fold into a closed cube, then check. Look for what the ones that work have in common.

What you are trying to notice: not every arrangement of six squares is a cube net — the faces have to be placed so that, when folded, they cover all six sides with none overlapping.

Arrangement 1 of 6

Carry this forward: there are exactly eleven different nets of a cube. Knowing which arrangements close, and why, is the same reasoning you will use to check a net before you build any solid.

🎨 Creativity — Design your own cube net

What to do: tap squares on the grid to design your own net of a cube — you need exactly six, joined edge to edge. Then fold-test it. Can you find nets nobody else would think of?

What you are trying to notice: there is more than one right answer — eleven, in fact — so designing a net is a genuine act of creation, not copying.

🌙 Rest — A pause between flat and solid

Holding a flat shape and a solid in mind at the same time — seeing one become the other — is real spatial thinking. It is tiring, good work. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: when you folded the net, which single face were you most sure about, and which did you have to think hardest to place? There is no right answer — just what your own mind found easy and hard.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. This lesson is hiding in your kitchen cupboard: every cardboard box is a net folded into a solid.

Why it matters: a cereal box is the net of a cuboid; a Toblerone box is the net of a triangular prism. A packaging designer draws the flat net first — faces, flaps and fold lines — then it is cut, creased and folded by machine. Getting the net right means no gaps, no overlaps and the least cardboard used. The reasoning you did today is exactly a packaging designer's job.

Look at a food box before you recycle it: can you spot the fold lines, and picture it flat as a net?

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show that you can move between a net and its solid, both ways.

  • Draw the net of a cube and of one other solid (a cuboid, a triangular prism, or a square-based pyramid). Label how many faces each has.
  • On your cube net, mark two pairs of faces that will end up opposite each other when folded.
  • Explain, in your own words, why the area of a net and the surface area of the solid it makes are always the same.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photo of a net you have drawn or folded, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
