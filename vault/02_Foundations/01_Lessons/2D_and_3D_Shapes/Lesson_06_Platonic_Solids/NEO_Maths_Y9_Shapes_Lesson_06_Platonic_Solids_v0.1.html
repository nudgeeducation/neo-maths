🌿 Connection — The count that never changes

🔤 New words: the five Platonic solids

A Platonic solid is a 3D shape where every face is the same regular polygon and the same number of faces meet at every corner. There are only five. They are named after the Greek philosopher Plato (around 360 BC), who linked them to the building blocks of the world — earth, air, fire, water, and the heavens. Nearly two thousand years later, Leonhard Euler — the mathematician in the film below — found the hidden count that ties all five together: F + V − E = 2.

What to do: watch the short film about Leonhard Euler, then use the table to work out F + V − E for each of the five solids. Look for what stays the same.

What you are trying to notice: however different the five solids look, the count faces + vertices − edges is always the same — a hidden invariant Euler discovered.

Carry this forward: F + V − E = 2 for every one of these solids — in fact for every convex polyhedron. It is called Euler's formula, and it is one of the threads tying this whole unit together.

🪞 Reflection — Why exactly five?

What to do: choose a regular polygon and how many meet at one corner of a solid. The tool adds up the angles. A corner can only fold into 3D if the angles leave a gap — they must total less than 360°.

What you are trying to notice: only five combinations leave a gap — and those are exactly the five Platonic solids. This is the same corner-fitting idea from the angles lesson, now in 3D.

Carry this forward: three, four or five triangles; three squares; three pentagons — five gaps, five solids. Six triangles, four squares or three hexagons make exactly 360° and lie flat (a tiling, not a solid), and anything more is impossible. That is the whole proof, in one picture.

🎨 Creativity — Solid detective, and hidden partners

What to do: identify each solid from its clues. Then discover the dual hidden inside every Platonic solid — join the centres of its faces and another Platonic solid appears.

What you are trying to notice: the five solids are not five separate facts — they pair up, and each contains its partner.

🌙 Rest — A pause among the perfect solids

These five shapes have fascinated people for thousands of years — Plato linked them to earth, air, fire, water and the cosmos. Sitting with something beautiful and complete is worth doing.

Notice one thing: of the five, which one do you find most pleasing to look at? There is no mathematical right answer — just the one that draws your eye.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. This lesson is in every games cupboard: the dice used for board games and role-playing — the d4, d6, d8, d12 and d20 — are exactly the five Platonic solids.

Why it matters: a die must be fair — every face equally likely — which means every face and every corner has to be identical. Only the five Platonic solids can do that, so those are the only fair dice with flat faces. The ordinary cube (d6) is one; the twenty-sided d20 is the icosahedron. Fairness in a game rests on the same mathematics you have explored today.

Next time you roll a die, you are holding a Platonic solid. Which one?

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show that you understand the five solids and why the list stops at five.

  • For each of the five Platonic solids, record: its name, the shape of its faces, and its numbers of faces, vertices and edges. Check that F + V − E = 2 for each.
  • Explain, in your own words, why there are exactly five — using the idea of the angles at a corner needing to be less than 360°.
  • Name one pair of dual solids and say how they are related.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written table, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
