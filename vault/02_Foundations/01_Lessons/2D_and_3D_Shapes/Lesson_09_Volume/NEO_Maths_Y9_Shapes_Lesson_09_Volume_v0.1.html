Volume

Driving Question: How much space does a solid fill?

Lesson type: Measurement — the amount of 3D space inside a solid.

Stepping Stone: find the volume of cuboids, prisms and cylinders — the space they fill, measured in cubic units (cm³).

🌱 Open Learner Guide 📘 Open Supporting Adult Guidance
🌿 Connection — Build volume as area × length

🔤 New words for this lesson

Volume — the amount of 3D space a solid fills, measured in cubic units (cm³).
Cubic centimetre (cm³) — the space filled by a cube 1 cm on every side.
Capacity — how much a container can hold; 1 litre = 1000 cm³.

What to do: choose a cross-section, read its area, then drag the Length slider. Watch the volume grow as area × length.

What you are trying to notice: a prism is a cross-section carried along a length — so its volume is simply the cross-section area × length. A cuboid and a cylinder are just special cases.

🧱 Prism Volume Machine

Carry this forward: volume of any prism = cross-section area × length. For a cuboid that is length × width × height; for a cylinder it is π r² × height.

🚶 Movement — Stack it up in unit cubes

What to do: set the base (how many cubes wide and deep) and the number of layers, and watch the solid fill with 1 cm cubes. Then drag Lean to slide the layers sideways.

What you are trying to notice: volume = cubes in one layer × number of layers = base area × height. And leaning the stack over does not change the volume — the same cubes are still there.

🧊 Layer-Stacker

Carry this forward: volume is one layer × the number of layers — that is base area × height, and it is exactly cross-section area × length. Leaning the stack (an oblique prism) keeps the same volume.

🪞 Reflection — Spot the slip

What to do: each card shows a short volume calculation. Decide whether it is right or has a slip in it, then reveal the reasoning.

What you are trying to notice: volume goes wrong in a few predictable ways — the wrong units, a diameter used as a radius, or adding when you should multiply.

🔍 Right or slip?

Carry this forward: volume is always in cubic units (cm³), uses the radius for circles, and comes from multiplying area by length — never adding.

🎨 Creativity — How much does it hold?

What to do: pick a real container, read its size, and work out its volume — how much space it holds. Predict, then check. Meet litres along the way.

What you are trying to notice: volume is a real quantity — the amount a container holds — and 1 litre is just 1000 cm³.

🫙 Capacity calculator

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, design a container to hold exactly 1 litre (1000 cm³). What dimensions could it have?

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Filling space with cubes, and seeing area become volume, is a real stretch of the imagination. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: did it surprise you that leaning the stack of layers kept the same volume? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — Where does this show up in the real world?

Mode: contextual. Volume is how much a thing holds — from a bottle of water to a swimming pool.

Why it matters: every label that says "500 ml" or "2 litres" is a volume. A tank's fuel, a pool's water, the concrete for a foundation, the dose in a syringe — all are volumes, and mistakes are costly. Shipping and packaging firms work hard to fit the most into each box, because wasted space costs money. Even a recipe scaled up uses volume. The area × length idea you built today is exactly how all of these are worked out.

Next time you pour a drink, picture the cubic centimetres — 1 litre is a cube 10 cm on each side.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: find and explain volumes.

  • Work out the volume of a cuboid and one other solid (a prism or a cylinder). Show the cross-section area and the length, and multiply.
  • Explain, in your own words, why the volume of any prism is cross-section area × length.
  • Find a real container at home, measure or estimate it, and work out roughly how much it holds in cm³ (and litres, if you like).

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written solution, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
