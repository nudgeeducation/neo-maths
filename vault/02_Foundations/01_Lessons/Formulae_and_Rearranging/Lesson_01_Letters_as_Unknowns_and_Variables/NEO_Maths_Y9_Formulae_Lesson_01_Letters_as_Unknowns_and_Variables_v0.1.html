NEO Mathematics: Formulae and Rearranging

Letters as unknowns and variables

Driving Question: What can a letter stand for?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the unit. One letter, several different jobs.

Stepping Stone: tell a letter used as an unknown from a letter used as a variable; recognise an expression, an equation, a formula and an identity; and read and write algebra in the usual notation (3n, ab, n², n/2).

You will need: the order of operations (multiply before you add) — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — A hidden number or a moving number?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

In algebra a letter stands for a number. Sometimes it is one particular number that we have not found yet: an unknown. Sometimes it can be many different numbers, and changes along with other quantities: a variable. The letter looks the same either way. What it is doing depends on the statement it is in.

What to do: on the left, slide n to find the number hidden in the box. On the right, stretch the rectangle and watch its perimeter. Then decide which letters are unknowns and which are variables.

What you are trying to notice: only one value of n opens the box. In the rectangle, l, w and P can take many values, and P changes whenever l or w does.

📦 The sealed box

n + 5 = 12

▭ The stretchy rectangle

P = 2l + 2w

Unknown or variable?

Carry this forward: an unknown is one particular number to be found; a variable can take many values, and changes with the other quantities in a formula.

🚶 Movement — The notation decoder

What to do: algebra has a short way of writing things. For each card, work out its value for the given number, then type it in. In the second half, write each long version the short way.

What you are trying to notice: 3n means 3 × n; n² means n × n; n/2 means n ÷ 2; and a power belongs only to what it is attached to. Reading the notation correctly is half of every substitution.

🔍 Decode, then encode

Carry this forward: write the number first (4n, not n4), leave out the × sign, and use a power for a letter multiplied by itself.

🪞 Reflection — When is it true?

What to do: slide the letter and watch the light. Try several values — including 0 and a negative number. Then decide what kind of statement it is: an equation, a formula, an expression or an identity.

What you are trying to notice: an equation is true for particular values only. An identity is true for every value. A formula links quantities, so one is worked out from the others. An expression has no equals sign: it has a value, but it cannot be true or false.

💡 Test the statement

What kind of statement is it?

Carry this forward: to decide what kind of statement you have, ask when it is true: for one value, for every value, as a link between quantities — or is there nothing to be true at all?

🎨 Creativity — Make one of each

What to do: write your own expression, equation, identity and formula. The tester tries lots of values and tells you what kind of statement you have written. Use × or just write letters next to numbers, and ^2 or ² for a square.

What you are trying to notice: an equation needs a value that makes it true, and an identity must survive every value. The tester is strict: one value that breaks an identity is enough.

🧪 The statement tester

✨ Make your own stretch

Write an identity that does not look obviously true — for example, one with brackets on one side only. Then write an equation that is true for two different values. Test both, and explain on the Scratchpad how you built them.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A single letter has been doing four different jobs today. That is a lot to ask of one small symbol. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: think of a letter you use every day — the first letter of your name. It means something different in different words, but it is always the same letter. Algebra works the same way.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds precision. Saying exactly what kind of statement you are making — a fact that is always true, a question with one answer, or a rule that links things — is useful far beyond mathematics. Scientists, coders and lawyers all depend on knowing whether a statement is always true or only sometimes.

Carry one idea forward: before you answer, ask what kind of question you have been asked.

🛠️ Final Task — A letter's four jobs

Your task: show that you can tell the four kinds of statement apart, and explain how you know.

  • Sort these, giving a reason for each: 5t − 2 = 18, v = u + at, 4(k + 1) = 4k + 4, 7p − p², y² = 9.
  • For each equation, find every value that makes it true. For the identity, test three values, including a negative one.
  • Write 3 × m × m, a × 5 × b and h ÷ 4 in the usual notation.
  • Explain the mistake in this working: "when n = 4, 3n = 34".
  • stretch Find a formula in real life — on a bill, a recipe or a sports table — and say which letters are variables and what each stands for.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
