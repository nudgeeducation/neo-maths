NEO Mathematics: Formulae and Rearranging

Formulae from words and diagrams

Driving Question: How do I turn a description into a formula?

Lesson type: Core — turning words and pictures into formulae, and then using them.

Stepping Stone: write a formula from a description in words or from a diagram, choosing letters for the quantities; and use the formula to answer questions.

You will need: writing simple expressions such as 5b + 30, and substituting values from Lesson 2 — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — Words to bars to formula

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Many everyday rules have the same shape: a fixed amount you always pay, plus an amount for each mile, month or hour. A bar model shows that shape, and a formula says it in a few symbols: a taxi costing £3 plus £2 a mile becomes C = 3 + 2m.

What to do: tap each phrase in the description to see what it adds to the bar, and what it will become in the formula. Move the slider to see the bar grow. Then put the pieces together: write the formula yourself, and use it.

What you are trying to notice: the fixed part is drawn once, whatever the slider says. The "for every" part is drawn again for each mile — so it becomes a number × letter term.

🧱 Build the formula

Carry this forward: find the fixed part and the "for each" part. The fixed part is a number on its own; the "for each" part is a number × the letter.

🚶 Movement — Walk round the shape

What to do: each shape has sides labelled with letters and numbers. Write a formula for what is asked — usually the perimeter. If you are unsure, press Walk round the shape to add the sides one at a time.

What you are trying to notice: a perimeter is every side added once. Equal sides collect together: x + x is 2x.

📐 Formula from a diagram

Carry this forward: go round the shape once, write every side, then collect the like terms.

🪞 Reflection — Word-order traps

What to do: choose the formula that matches each description. Then look at the table: it compares the description with its "twin", which uses the same words in a different order.

What you are trying to notice: the order of the words is the order of the operations. "Double, then add 3" and "add 3, then double" use the same numbers but give different answers.

🔀 Same words, different order

Carry this forward: read the description in order, and do the operations in that order. Brackets show what must happen first.

🎨 Creativity — Which plan is cheaper?

What to do: three gyms describe their prices in words. Write a formula for the cost of n months at each one. Then use your formulae to decide which gym is cheapest — and when the answer changes.

What you are trying to notice: a formula lets you answer "what if?" questions for any number of months, without working out every case by hand. The cheapest choice depends on how long you stay.

🏋️ Three gyms

✨ Make your own stretch

Design a fourth gym price that is cheapest for exactly 6 to 10 months. Write it in words and as a formula, and check it against the other three.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A sentence became a picture, and the picture became a few symbols. That is a lot of translating. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: think of something you pay for — a bus pass, a phone, a game. Is there a fixed part and a "for each" part? You may already be using a formula without writing it down.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds modelling — turning a real situation into mathematics, answering a question with it, and turning the answer back into words. Comparing price plans is something adults do all the time, often without the tools to do it well.

Carry one idea forward: when a choice depends on "how many" or "how long", a formula can show you the whole picture.

🛠️ Final Task — From words to a decision

Your task: show that you can write formulae from words and diagrams, and use them to make a decision.

  • Write a formula for each: a plumber who charges £45 to call out plus £30 an hour; a candle that is 20 cm tall and burns 2 cm each hour; the perimeter of a rectangle that is x cm long and 5 cm wide.
  • Use each formula once: the cost of 2½ hours, the height after 6 hours, the perimeter when x = 8.
  • Two phone plans: Plan A costs £12 a month; Plan B costs £30 to start and then £9 a month. Write a formula for each, and find when Plan B becomes cheaper.
  • Explain the difference between "add 4, then multiply by 3" and "multiply by 3, then add 4", using formulae and one example.
  • stretch Find a real price list (a cinema, a taxi, a hire company), write its formula, and say what each letter and number means.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
