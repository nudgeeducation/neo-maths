NEO Mathematics: Formulae and Rearranging

Deriving a formula

Driving Question: Can I find the rule myself, and say why it works?

Lesson type: Core — finding formulae for yourself, from patterns and shapes, and explaining why they are right.

Stepping Stone: derive a formula from a pattern or a shape by finding what stays the same and what grows; test it on a case you have not drawn; and explain why it works, using the structure of the picture.

You will need: linear sequences and the nth term, and writing formulae from Lesson 3 — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — The rule detective

🔤 The idea for this lesson

To derive a formula is to find it for yourself and be able to say why it is right. A table of numbers can suggest a rule. A picture of the structure can explain it: this part stays the same in every step, and this part grows by the same amount each time.

What to do: each machine hides a formula. Use its table to find the formula, then test your formula on new inputs and see whether the machine agrees.

What you are trying to notice: how much the output goes up each time tells you the number in front of n. A table tells you what the rule is — but not why. The rest of the lesson is about the why.

🚶 Movement — Net to formula

What to do: a cuboid has edges a, b and c. Tap each face of its net to add its area to the surface area. Then write the formula the short way, and use it on a real box.

What you are trying to notice: the faces come in matching pairs — that is where the 2s in the formula come from.

📦 Unfold the box

Carry this forward: a formula from a shape is the sum of its parts, written as simply as possible — and each part can be pointed at.

🪞 Reflection — Two ways to see it (and more)

What to do: this is a border of tiles round an n × n square. Each picture groups the tiles in a different way. Choose the formula that matches each grouping.

What you are trying to notice: different ways of seeing the same pattern give formulae that look different but always give the same answer. They are identities — the same idea as in Lesson 1.

👀 Four ways of seeing one border

Carry this forward: if two formulae come from correct ways of seeing the same thing, they must agree for every n. That is a powerful way to check your algebra.

🎨 Creativity — The pattern builder

What to do: move the slider to build the pattern step by step, and count. Press Colour the structure to see what stays the same and what grows. Write a formula for step n, test it on step 50, and explain it. Then design a pattern of your own.

What you are trying to notice: the gold part appears once in every step; the blue part is added again with each step. Gold gives the number on its own, blue gives the number × n — and that is why the formula works for every step, even ones you will never draw.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You found formulae for yourself today, and you can point at every part of them. That is real mathematics. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: step 50 of a pattern is too big to draw, yet you know exactly how many pieces it has. Picture it stretching away — and know that your formula has already counted it.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds proof — knowing that something is always true, not just true for the cases you checked. Seeing why a formula works, from the structure, is the first step towards the proofs mathematicians write.

Carry one idea forward: when a rule works, ask why. The answer is usually hiding in the picture.

🛠️ Final Task — Derive it, test it, explain it

Your task: show that you can derive formulae from patterns and shapes, and explain why they work.

  • A pattern of houses is made from rods: one house uses 5 rods, two joined houses use 9, three use 13. Draw steps 1–3, colour the part that stays the same, and derive a formula for the number of rods in step n.
  • Use your formula to find the rods in step 30, and to find which step uses 81 rods.
  • Explain, by pointing at your drawing, what each number in your formula represents.
  • Derive a formula for the surface area of a square-based box with base edge s and height h, and test it with s = 3, h = 5.
  • stretch Find a second way of seeing your house pattern that gives a formula that looks different, and show that the two formulae always agree.
