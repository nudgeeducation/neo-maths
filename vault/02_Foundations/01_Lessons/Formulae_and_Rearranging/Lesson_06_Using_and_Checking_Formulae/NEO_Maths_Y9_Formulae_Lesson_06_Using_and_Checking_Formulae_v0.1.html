NEO Mathematics: Formulae and Rearranging

Using and checking formulae in context

Driving Question: Does my answer make sense?

Lesson type: Core — the last lesson of the unit. Using formulae to solve real problems, and checking the answers.

Stepping Stone: use formulae in context: decide whether to substitute or rearrange first, make the units consistent, and check that the answer is reasonable — including by substituting it back.

You will need: converting units of length, time and money, and Lessons 2–5 of this unit — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — The sense detector

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A formula always gives a number. Whether that number is right depends on choosing the right formula, using consistent units, and working accurately. The quickest check of all is common sense: does the answer fit the real world?

What to do: each card is an answer from someone's homework. Decide whether it is believable. If it is not, find the most likely slip.

What you are trying to notice: most unbelievable answers come from the same few slips: a decimal point in the wrong place, mixed units, or multiplying when you should divide.

🚨 Believable or not?

Carry this forward: before you trust an answer, picture it. Then check the units and the operation.

🚶 Movement — Units first

What to do: each formula needs its values in matching units. Choose the conversion that makes the units match, then work out the answer. Press What if I don't convert? to see what goes wrong.

What you are trying to notice: a speed in km per hour needs a time in hours; an area in m² needs lengths in metres. Converting first keeps the formula honest.

📏 Make the units match

Carry this forward: check the units of every value before you substitute — and the units the answer must be in.

🪞 Reflection — The decision trail

What to do: each problem is a short trail of decisions. At each stop, choose — then calculate, and finish by checking your answer. A wrong turn shows you where it would have led.

What you are trying to notice: the same few questions solve almost every formula problem: What do I know? What do I need? Substitute or rearrange? Are the units consistent? Does the answer make sense — and does it fit when I put it back in?

🧭 Choose, calculate, check

Carry this forward: know, need, route, units, calculate, check. Six small decisions — and the check is never optional.

🎨 Creativity — Plan a day out

What to do: two of you are planning a trip to a museum with a budget of £40. Use the formulae to cost each part, check the total, and change the plan if it does not fit.

What you are trying to notice: several formulae can work together to answer one real question — and checking the total against the budget is part of the answer.

🗺️ The trip

✨ Make your own stretch

Plan a day out of your own with three formulae — travel, entry and food — and a budget. Write each formula, cost each part, and check the total. Then ask someone to check your answers with the decision trail.

🌙 Rest — A pause to close the unit

This unit began with a single letter and ends with real decisions. You have written formulae, derived them, turned them round and checked them. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: next time you see a price list, a recipe or a timetable, notice the formula hiding inside it. You can now read it, write it — and check it.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds judgement — the habit of asking whether an answer makes sense before you rely on it. Calculators, spreadsheets and apps all produce numbers confidently, even wrong ones. A person who checks is worth a great deal.

Carry one idea forward: an answer is not finished until it has been checked.

🛠️ Final Task — Solve it, check it

Your task: show that you can use formulae to solve real problems, and check your answers.

  • A runner covers 5 km in 25 minutes. Use s = d/t to find the speed in km per hour. Show the unit conversion.
  • A plumber charges C = 40 + 25h pounds. A bill is £152.50. How many hours did the job take? Say whether you substituted or rearranged first, and why.
  • A rectangle of card is 1.2 m by 80 cm. Find its area in m², and in cm².
  • For each answer, write one sentence saying why it is sensible, and check it by substituting back.
  • stretch Find a real formula problem (a recipe, a bill, a journey), solve it, and explain one place where a unit slip could have caused a silly answer.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
