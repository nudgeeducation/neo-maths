NEO Mathematics: Inequalities

Inequality symbols and double-ended inequalities

Driving Question: Who gets through the gate?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the unit, where an answer stops being one number and becomes a whole set of numbers with a boundary.

Stepping Stone: read and use the symbols <, >, ≤ and ≥, including double-ended inequalities such as −1 < x ≤ 3; decide whether a boundary value is included; list the whole numbers that satisfy an inequality; and turn a real-world boundary into an inequality.

You will need: ordering numbers, including negative numbers, on a number line — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🌿 Connection — Which is greater?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Until now, most questions have had one answer: x = 5. But real life is full of boundaries: you must be at least 12 to ride; a bag must weigh less than 7 kg. A boundary does not pick out one number — it lets a whole set of numbers through, and keeps others out. The language of boundaries is the inequality, and it starts with four symbols: < (is less than), > (is greater than), ≤ (is less than or equal to) and ≥ (is greater than or equal to).

What to do: drag the two points a and b along the number line (or use the arrow buttons). Then choose the symbol that makes a __ b true. Use Swap the order to write the same fact the other way round.

What you are trying to notice: on a number line, numbers get greater as you move to the right — even negative ones, so −2 is greater than −5. And the same fact can be written two ways: a < b says exactly what b > a says. The open side of the symbol always faces the greater number.

⚖️ Build the comparison

Carry this forward: further right means greater. −2 > −5 and −5 < −2 are the same fact, read from different ends.

🚶 Movement — Catch the whole numbers

What to do: read the rule, then tap every whole number (an integer: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …) on the line that obeys it. Press Check when you think you have them all.

What you are trying to notice: the two boundaries decide everything. A ≤ or ≥ lets its boundary number in; a < or > keeps it out. So −2 ≤ n < 3 includes −2 but not 3.

🎯 Tap the whole numbers that get in

Carry this forward: list the whole numbers by starting at the lower boundary and stepping up to the upper one — then check each boundary: in or out?

🪞 Reflection — Who gets through the gate?

What to do: the gate has a rule. Numbers arrive one at a time. For each one, decide Through or Not through, then choose the reason. Every decision you get right is marked on the number line below.

What you are trying to notice: your decisions are drawing the answer. When every number has been sorted, the numbers that got through all sit on one stretch of the line — that stretch is the inequality. The boundary numbers are the hardest decisions, and they decide whether the end of the stretch is an open circle ○ (left out) or a filled circle ● (let in).

🚪 The boundary gate

Carry this forward: an inequality describes a set of numbers. Test any number by putting it into the rule: is the statement true? At a boundary, the symbol decides — ≤ ≥ include it, < > leave it out.

🎨 Creativity — Boundaries in the real world

What to do: each sentence describes a real boundary. Build it as an inequality by choosing the symbols and numbers, then press Check. The allowed values appear on a scale.

What you are trying to notice: everyday words carry the symbol. At least and no less than mean ≥; at most and no more than mean ≤; more than and above mean >; less than and below mean <. "Between … including both" gives a double-ended inequality with two filled circles.

✨ Make your own boundary stretch

Think of a real boundary from your own life — a game, a recipe, a journey, a temperature. Write it in words, then build it. There is no right answer: the line shows exactly what your rule allows.

🌙 Rest — A pause at the boundary

Boundaries are everywhere, and most of them are quiet: the volume you keep below a certain level, the time you need to leave by, the temperature you like your room. Take a slow breath and let today's idea settle.

Notice one thing: think of one boundary in your own day. Is its boundary value allowed or not allowed — would it be ≤ or <? There is no right answer; just notice it.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds precision about limits. Safety rules, fair rules and health guidance are all boundaries — a maximum speed, a minimum age, a safe dose — and the difference between "up to" and "less than" can matter. Being able to read and write a boundary exactly is a way of looking after yourself and others, and of noticing when a rule is unclear.

Carry one idea forward: whenever you meet a limit, ask "is the boundary itself allowed?"

🛠️ Final Task — Design three gates

Your task: design three gates, each with a rule from real life.

  • Write each rule in words and as an inequality. Use at least one ≤ or ≥ and at least one < or >.
  • Make one of your rules double-ended, like 18 ≤ t ≤ 22.
  • For each gate, give two numbers that get through and two that do not — including a boundary number — and explain each decision.
  • For your double-ended gate, list all the whole numbers that get through, if there are any.
  • stretch Use a negative boundary, such as a temperature below freezing, and explain how you decided which numbers are greater.
