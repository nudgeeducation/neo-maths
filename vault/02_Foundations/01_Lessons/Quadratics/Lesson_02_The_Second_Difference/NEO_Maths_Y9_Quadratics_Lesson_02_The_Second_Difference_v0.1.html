The second difference

Driving Question: How can one constant number reveal a hidden quadratic?

🌿 Connection — The fingerprint

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Last lesson you noticed that a growing pattern's gaps (the first differences) keep getting bigger. Today we chase the question you were left with: amid all that growing, is anything constant? Take the sequence 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. The first differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 — growing. Now take the differences of those: 5−3, 7−5, 9−7 — every one is 2. That is the second difference, and for a quadratic it is always the same number. This constant second difference is the true fingerprint of a quadratic.

A linear pattern has a constant first difference. A quadratic pattern has a constant second difference. That one fact will let you spot a quadratic, continue it, and — soon — connect it to its rule.

What to do: pick a sequence, then press Reveal the next row to fill in the first differences, and again for the second differences.

What you are trying to notice: the first differences grow, but the second differences settle to a single, repeating number.

🔍 The fingerprint

Carry this forward: constant first difference → linear. Constant second difference → quadratic.

🚶 Movement — Second-Difference Detective

What to do: the last term is hidden. Use the constant second difference to work back up: it gives the next first difference, which gives the next term. Press Show the differences to see the rows, then type the next term.

What you are trying to notice: the second difference is a tool, not just a label. Add it to the last gap to get the next gap; add that to the last term to get the next term. You can continue any quadratic this way.

🕵️ Second-Difference Detective

Carry this forward: next gap = last gap + the constant second difference; next term = last term + next gap.

🪞 Reflection — Is it quadratic?

What to do: each card shows a sequence. Press Show the differences, then decide: is it linear, quadratic, or neither?

What you are trying to notice: the second-difference test sorts sequences cleanly. A constant first difference means linear. A constant second difference means quadratic. If the second differences are not constant, it is neither — something else is going on.

🔎 Is it quadratic?

Carry this forward: constant 1st difference → linear · constant 2nd difference → quadratic · neither constant → something else.

🎨 Creativity — The second difference and the rule

What to do: choose a number a, and look at the sequence made by the rule y = a n² (that is, a lots of the square numbers). Predict its second difference, then reveal it.

What you are trying to notice: the second difference is not random — it is 2 × a, twice the number in front of n². The plain square numbers (a = 1) have second difference 2; double them (a = 2) and the second difference doubles to 4. The fingerprint is tied directly to the rule.

🧬 The second difference and the rule

Make it your own: on the Scratchpad, write out y = 4n² for n = 1 to 5, take the differences twice, and check the second difference really is 2 × 4 = 8.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have found the steady thing hiding underneath a pattern that would not stop changing. That is a quietly satisfying discovery — take a breath with it before moving on.

Notice one thing: when you took the differences a second time and the same number kept appearing, did it feel surprising, or expected? There is no right answer — just what your own attention noticed.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. Difference tables are honest mathematical tools, not the nourishment itself — so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the deep idea of an invariant — something that stays perfectly constant while everything on the surface changes. The terms grew, the gaps grew, yet the second difference sat there, unchanging, quietly holding the whole pattern together. Mathematicians hunt for invariants everywhere, because a thing that does not change is a thing you can rely on, name and use. You did more than spot one today — you used it to predict, and tied it to a rule. Learning to ask "what is invariant here?" is one of the most powerful questions in all of mathematics and science.

Carry one idea forward: when something keeps changing, keep taking differences — the constant, when it appears, is the pattern's true signature.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show that you can use the second difference to identify and continue a quadratic.

  • Take a quadratic sequence. Build its full difference table — terms, first differences, second differences — and point to the constant second difference.
  • Use that constant second difference to work out the next two terms, showing the working (next gap, then next term).
  • Sort three sequences into linear, quadratic and neither, explaining each decision using the differences.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a written difference table, a labelled poster, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
