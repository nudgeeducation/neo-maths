Missing lengths in similar shapes

Driving Question: How does the scale factor find a missing length?

Lesson type: Core — using the scale factor between similar triangles to find missing lengths.

Stepping Stone: recognise similar triangles from two pairs of equal angles; find the scale factor from one pair of corresponding sides whose lengths are both known; use it to find a missing length, in separate triangles and in triangles one inside the other.

You will need: Lesson 7 (similar shapes), and multiplying by fractions — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad.

🔤 The idea for this lesson

In similar shapes, every length is multiplied by the same scale factor. Find it from one pair of corresponding sides whose lengths you know both of: a length of the second shape over the matching length of the first.

Then multiply any length of the first shape by the scale factor to find the matching length of the second.

🚶 Movement — The scale-factor finder (today's main activity)

What to do: for each problem, choose a pair of corresponding sides whose lengths are both known, build the scale factor as a fraction, then find the missing length.

What you are trying to notice: one known pair fixes the scale factor for every pair. Which length goes over which decides whether the answer grows or shrinks. When one triangle sits inside another, use whole sides, not the extra pieces.

🪞 Reflection — Spot the slip

What to do: each working has one slip. Find the line it is in, then read why.

What you are trying to notice: the slips that are easy to make: the fraction upside down, adding instead of multiplying, using a piece instead of a whole side, and matching the wrong sides.

🎨 Creativity — The shadow measurer

What to do: set your height, the length of your shadow and the length of a tree's shadow, measured at the same moment. Then work out how tall the tree is.

What you are trying to notice: the sun's rays meet the ground at the same angle at you and at the tree, so the two triangles are similar.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

One pair of sides tells you the scale factor for all of them. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: if you are outside, or near a window, look at the shadows of two upright things. Do the longer shadows belong to the taller things?

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: practical. Today the mathematics feeds measuring what you cannot reach.

What it feeds: a story from ancient Greece tells of Thales finding the height of a pyramid from its shadow and the shadow of a stick, with exactly today's similar triangles. Surveyors still measure the heights of buildings and the widths of rivers this way, and a camera lens makes a small image that is similar to the scene in front of it.

Carry one idea forward: one known pair of corresponding sides gives the scale factor for every pair.

🛠️ Final Task — Find it, then check it

Your task: make a pair of similar triangles, use the scale factor to predict a missing length, then measure to check.

  • Draw a triangle, and a line parallel to one of its sides that cuts the other two sides, making a small triangle inside the big one.
  • Explain why the two triangles are similar.
  • Measure one pair of corresponding sides, and write the scale factor as a fraction.
  • Use it to predict the length of another side, then measure to check.
  • stretch Find the length of one of the extra pieces, and explain why you cannot use it to find the scale factor.
