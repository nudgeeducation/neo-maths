NEO Mathematics: Algebraic Expressions and Identities

Expressions, equations, formulae and identities

Driving Question: Is it true for some values, or for every value?

Lesson type: Core — telling statements apart, and proving that two expressions are always equal.

Stepping Stone: distinguish an expression, an equation, a formula and an identity, and use the symbol ≡; explain why testing values can suggest an identity but only algebra can prove one; use a counterexample to show a statement is not an identity; and find missing numbers in an identity.

You will need: substitution, including negative numbers, and expanding a bracket — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — The identity tester

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Some statements are true only for particular values: 3x + 2 = 14 is true only when x = 4. That is an equation. Others are true for every value: 2(x + 3) and 2x + 6 are always equal. That is an identity, written with ≡: 2(x + 3) ≡ 2x + 6. Testing values can make us suspect an identity. Only algebra can prove it.

What to do: move the slider to try different values of x, or test 21 values at once. Watch both sides and the graph. Then decide: identity or equation? Prove it, or find a counterexample.

What you are trying to notice: for an identity, the two graphs are exactly the same line, one drawn over the other. For an equation, they meet only where the statement is true.

🪞 Reflection — The counterexample hunt

What to do: each claim says two expressions are always equal. Try to break it: type a value of x where the two sides are different. If you think it can't be broken, say so.

What you are trying to notice: one counterexample is enough to show a claim is false. But a value where the sides agree proves nothing on its own.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Two different-looking expressions, and one line on the graph. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: there are infinitely many numbers, and you can only ever test a few. Yet a few lines of algebra can tell you about all of them at once.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds healthy scepticism. “It worked every time I tried it” is not the same as “it is always true”. Knowing the difference — and knowing that a single counterexample can sink a confident claim — is useful far beyond mathematics.

Carry one idea forward: when someone says “always”, ask how they know.

🛠️ Final Task — Always, sometimes, never

Your task: show that you can tell statements apart, prove an identity and disprove a false one.

  • Say whether each is an expression, an equation, a formula or an identity, and why: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12; 3x + 4 = 19; P = 2(l + w); 3x + 4.
  • Prove that (x + 4)² − 16 ≡ x(x + 8).
  • Find a counterexample to show that (x − 3)² = x² − 9 is not an identity. Explain why one counterexample is enough.
  • Find a and b so that a(x + b) ≡ 6x − 18.
  • stretch Explain why checking that two expressions agree at x = 0, 1, 2 and 3 does not prove they are identical. Use an example.
