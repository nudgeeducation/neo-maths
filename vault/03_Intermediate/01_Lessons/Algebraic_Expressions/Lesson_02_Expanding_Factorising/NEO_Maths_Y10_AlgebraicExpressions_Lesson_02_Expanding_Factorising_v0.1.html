NEO Mathematics: Algebraic Expressions and Identities

Expanding and factorising

Driving Question: How can one expression be written as a sum and as a product?

Lesson type: Core — reading one rectangle two ways: as a sum of areas, and as length × width.

Stepping Stone: expand single and double brackets, including negative terms and coefficients; factorise by taking out the highest common factor, including letters; factorise quadratics x² + bx + c, including negative numbers; and recognise the difference of two squares.

You will need: expanding a bracket, factor pairs, and multiplying negative numbers — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — Sum or product?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

The same expression can be written as a sum of terms or as a product of factors: x² + 7x + 12 ≡ (x + 3)(x + 4). Expanding turns a product into a sum. Factorising turns a sum back into a product. Picture a rectangle: its area is the sum of its parts, and also its length × its width.

What to do: decide whether each expression is written as a sum or as a product.

What you are trying to notice: a product is one thing multiplied by another. If a + or − sits outside every bracket, it is a sum.

➕ or ✖️?

Carry this forward: expanded means a sum of terms; fully factorised means one product.

🚶 Movement — The two-way area grid

What to do: in Expand tasks, the sides of the rectangle are given: fill in each cell, then collect the total. In Factorise tasks, only the total is given: choose the sides, and the cells update as you type, until the total matches.

What you are trying to notice: for (x + p)(x + q), the last cell is pq and the two middle cells add to (p + q)x. So to factorise, you need two numbers that multiply to the number term and add to the x coefficient.

🔲 One rectangle, two ways

(x +)(x +)

stretch Why must the two numbers multiply to c and add to b? A pattern suggests — a proof convinces.

Carry this forward: (x + p)(x + q) ≡ x² + (p + q)x + pq. Always check a factorisation by expanding it.

🪞 Reflection — The factor-pair finder

What to do: here are the pairs of whole numbers that multiply to make the number term. Choose a pair, and see what it adds to. Find the pair that adds to the x coefficient.

What you are trying to notice: when the number term is negative, one number of the pair is negative. When the number term is positive but the x coefficient is negative, both are negative.

🔎 Multiply to …, add to …

Carry this forward: list the factor pairs (with signs), and test their sums. Then check by expanding.

🎨 Creativity — Which ones factorise?

What to do: find every value that makes the quadratic factorise into two brackets with whole numbers. Type one value at a time.

What you are trying to notice: each factor pair gives exactly one value. So the factor pairs tell you how many there are — and when you have found them all.

🧩 Find them all

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

One rectangle; two true descriptions. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: 12 can be 3 × 4 or 12 × 1 — and x² + 7x + 12 knows which one it wants. The middle term chooses.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds flexibility: the habit of seeing one thing in more than one way. A sum is good for adding up; a product is good for solving and simplifying. Choosing the right form for the job is a skill used throughout mathematics — and well beyond it.

Carry one idea forward: if a problem looks hard in one form, try writing it another way.

🛠️ Final Task — Both ways round

Your task: show that you can expand and factorise, and explain why the methods work.

  • Expand and simplify (3x − 2)(x + 4) and (x − 7)².
  • Factorise fully: 10x² − 15x; x² + 2x − 15; x² − 81.
  • Use an area grid to explain why x² + 7x + 10 = (x + 2)(x + 5).
  • Check each factorisation by expanding it.
  • stretch Prove that (x + p)(x + q) ≡ x² + (p + q)x + pq. Use it to explain why x² + 4x + 5 cannot be factorised with whole numbers.
