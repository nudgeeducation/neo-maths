Algebraic proof

Driving Question: How can I be sure it is true for every number?

Lesson type: Core — from "it worked every time I tried" to "it must always work".

Stepping Stone: write general numbers in algebra — even 2n, odd 2n + 1, consecutive n, n + 1, n + 2; prove statements for every whole number by expanding, simplifying and factorising; tell evidence, counterexample and proof apart; and finish a proof with a clear conclusion.

You will need: odd and even numbers, multiples, and expanding and factorising — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — Write it in algebra

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Checking examples can make us confident, but there are infinitely many numbers — we can never check them all. Algebra gets round this: if n stands for any whole number, then 2n is any even number and 2n + 1 is any odd number. Whatever we show about 2n + 1 is true for every odd number at once. That is a proof.

What to do: choose the algebra that describes each kind of number.

What you are trying to notice: the expression must work for every value of n — not just some.

🔤 Which expression?

Carry this forward: even 2n; odd 2n + 1; consecutive n, n + 1, n + 2; a multiple of 3 is 3 × (a whole number).

🚶 Movement — The proof jigsaw

What to do: the lines of a proof have been jumbled. Tap them in the right order.

What you are trying to notice: every proof has the same shape: say what the numbers are in algebra → work with them → show the result has the form you need → conclude.

🪞 Reflection — The proof critic

What to do: read each argument. Is it a proof — or just examples, or a proof with a mistake?

What you are trying to notice: examples are evidence, not proof. And a proof can still fail if one step is wrong, or if it quietly assumes too much.

🧐 Is it a proof?

🎨 Creativity — The conjecture lab

What to do: each claim is a conjecture — something that might be true. Test it with your own numbers. Decide: always, sometimes or never true? Then either give a counterexample, or build the proof.

What you are trying to notice: many examples can make a claim look certain — and still one number can break it. Only the algebra settles it for every number.

Notice one thing: a proof is a kind of promise. Once it is written, it holds for numbers no one will ever have time to check.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the power of a good argument. Proof is how mathematicians know things for certain — and the habit of asking “how do we know this is always true?” is valuable in science, in law, and in everyday life.

Carry one idea forward: “it worked every time I tried” is a reason to look for a proof, not a proof itself.

🛠️ Final Task — Prove it

Your task: show that you can test conjectures, disprove false ones and prove true ones.

  • Prove that the sum of any three consecutive whole numbers is a multiple of 3.
  • Prove that the product of any two odd numbers is odd.
  • Prove that the sum of two consecutive odd numbers is always a multiple of 4.
  • Show that “n² + n + 41 is always prime” is false. Explain why your example is enough.
  • stretch Prove that the difference between the squares of two consecutive odd numbers is always a multiple of 8.
