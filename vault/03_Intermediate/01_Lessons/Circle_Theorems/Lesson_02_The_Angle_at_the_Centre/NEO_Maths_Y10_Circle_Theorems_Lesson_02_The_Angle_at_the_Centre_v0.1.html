NEO Mathematics: Circle Theorems

The angle at the centre

Driving Question: Why is the angle at the middle always double the one at the edge?

Lesson type: Core — the keystone of the unit. Every later theorem is deduced from this one, so it is worth securing thoroughly.

Stepping Stone: discover, and then justify, that the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc — and use it to find missing angles, always giving the reason.

You will need: the parts of a circle and the idea of an angle standing on an arc (Lesson 1), that angles in a triangle sum to 180°, and that a triangle with two equal sides has two equal base angles (isosceles). Keep a Reconnection Route to isosceles triangles handy.

🌿 Connection — Two angles on the same arc

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Take two points A and B on a circle. They cut off an arc — and any angle that "stands on" that arc subtends it. There are two special places to stand. Stand at the centre O and you get the angle at the centre, ∠AOB, made by the two radii. Stand anywhere on the far circumference, at a point P, and you get an angle at the circumference, ∠APB, made by the two chords. Both stand on the same arc AB. This lesson is about the surprising, exact relationship between them — the single fact the rest of the unit is built on.

What to do: A and B are fixed. Drag P around the circle. Watch the two angles: ∠AOB at the centre (gold) and ∠APB at P (blue). Notice that both open onto the same arc AB.

What you are trying to notice: the angle at the centre and the angle at the circumference both subtend arc AB — but the centre angle looks bigger. How much bigger? Hold that question.

🔦 Centre angle and circumference angle

Carry this forward: the angle at the centre ∠AOB and the angle at the circumference ∠APB can stand on the same arc. Next we measure how they compare.

🚶 Movement — Half and double

What to do: once you know the rule — the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference on the same arc — you can find a missing angle in one step. For each diagram, work out the marked angle and type it in (in degrees). Give the reason too when asked.

What you are trying to notice: if you are given the centre angle, you halve it to get the circumference angle; if you are given the circumference angle, you double it to get the centre angle. Same rule, read both ways.

🧮 Find the missing angle

Carry this forward: centre = 2 × circumference; circumference = ½ × centre. Always name the theorem as your reason.

🪞 Reflection — The angle that will not change keystone

What to do: here is the heart of the lesson. A and B are fixed. Drag P slowly all the way around the major arc. Keep your eye on ∠APB at the circumference. Then press Reveal the ratio.

What you are trying to notice: two astonishing things. First, as P moves, ∠APB does not change — every point on that arc sees AB at the same angle. Second, that angle is exactly half the angle at the centre: ∠AOB = 2 × ∠APB. Drag A and B to a new arc and it still holds. This is the angle at the centre theorem.

🔬 The Circle Theorem Lab

Carry this forward: the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc, and every angle at the circumference on that arc is equal. That equality is the seed of the next lessons.

🎨 Creativity — Why is it exactly double? stretch

What to do: a rule you can prove is worth ten you just memorise. Press the steps to build the proof. The secret is a single extra line: join P to O and continue it, splitting the picture into two isosceles triangles.

What you are trying to notice: OA, OB and OP are all radii, so they are equal — which makes triangles OAP and OBP isosceles, each with two equal base angles (x and y). Because a triangle's angles sum to 180°, the apex angle of each is ∠AOP = 180 − 2x and ∠BOP = 180 − 2y. Then, since P, O and Q lie on a straight line, the angles at O add to 180°, so ∠AOQ = 2x and ∠BOQ = 2y. Add the two top pieces and the centre angle comes out as exactly twice the angle at P.

🧩 Build the proof

The reflex case: when P sits on the minor arc, the angle at the centre standing on the same arc is the reflex angle — and the theorem still holds: the reflex ∠AOB is twice ∠APB. Press Show the reflex case.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

You have found something that feels almost too neat to be true — an angle that refuses to change however you move the point, and a centre angle that is exactly, provably, double. Sit with that for a moment before moving on. The best mathematics often has this quality of quiet inevitability.

Notice one thing: which was more convincing for you — seeing the ratio hold as you dragged P, or proving it with the isosceles triangles? Both matter, and people differ. There is no right answer here.

You can jot it on the Scratchpad, say it aloud, or simply hold it in mind. You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. Circle geometry is abstract deductive mathematics, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the deep pleasure of an invariant — a quantity that stays fixed while everything around it moves. Noticing what doesn't change is one of the most powerful habits in all of mathematics and science, from conservation of energy to the constants of nature. And it feeds the satisfaction of a proof that closes: not "it looks double" but "it must be double, and here is why". One keystone fact, honestly justified, will now carry a whole family of results.

Carry one idea forward: when something surprising stays constant, ask what constraint is holding it — and then try to prove it.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show you can state, use and justify the angle-at-the-centre theorem.

  • Draw a circle with centre O and points A, B, P on it. Measure or mark ∠AOB and ∠APB, and state the relationship ∠AOB = 2 × ∠APB in words.
  • Given a circumference angle of your choice, work out the centre angle (and vice versa), writing the theorem as your reason each time.
  • Explain in your own words why the centre angle is double — using the equal radii, the isosceles triangles and the exterior-angle fact.
  • stretch Explain what happens to the centre angle when P is on the minor arc (the reflex case), and why the theorem still holds.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed hand-drawn diagram with both angles marked, a construction built and measured in the Geometry Studio (using Measure → Angle), a written proof, or a spoken/video explanation.
