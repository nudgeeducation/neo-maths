NEO Mathematics: Circle Theorems

Angles in the same segment

Driving Question: Why don't these angles change as the point slides round?

Lesson type: Core — a second consequence of the keystone: every point in the same segment sees a chord at the same angle.

Stepping Stone: discover, and then justify, that angles in the same segment are equal — two points on the same arc subtend a chord at the same angle — and use it (with the reason) to find missing angles.

You will need: the angle at the centre theorem (Lesson 2), the idea of an angle standing on an arc, and what a segment of a circle is (Lesson 1).

🌿 Connection — Two viewpoints, one angle

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A chord AB cuts a circle into two segments. Stand anywhere on the arc of one segment — at P, or at Q, or anywhere else on that same arc — and look back at the chord AB. The astonishing thing is that you always see it at the same angle. Why? Because every one of those angles at the circumference is half the same angle at the centre, ∠AOB. One central angle, so one shared half — and all the angles on that arc come out equal. This is the theorem: angles in the same segment are equal.

What to do: A and B are fixed. There are two points, P and Q, on the same arc — drag either one along the arc and watch the two angles ∠APB (blue) and ∠AQB (purple).

What you are trying to notice: the two angles stay equal, however you move P or Q, because both look back at the same chord from the same arc.

🪞 Reflection — The angle that follows you round keystone case

What to do: A and B are fixed, and P and Q sit on the same arc. Drag P and Q anywhere along that arc, watching both angles. Then press Why equal?.

What you are trying to notice: the two angles are always equal — move P close to A, move Q near the top, it makes no difference. And the reason is the keystone again: each angle is half of the one central angle ∠AOB, so they must match.

What you are trying to notice: the single central angle ∠AOB is shared by both P and Q. Each angle at the circumference is exactly half of it, so they cannot help being equal.

🧩 Build the proof

Looking ahead: what if P and Q are in different segments, on opposite sides of the chord? Then the angles are no longer equal — they add to 180°. That is the next lesson: cyclic quadrilaterals.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

An angle that follows you all the way round an arc, never changing. Notice, again, how last lesson's keystone has quietly done all the work — a third result for free. Take a breath before moving on.

Notice one thing: the three theorems so far — double at the centre, right angle in a semicircle, equal in the same segment — are really one idea seen from three positions. Does thinking of them that way make them easier to hold?

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. We name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds a sense of economy — the quiet delight of getting a whole new theorem from one you already have, with no extra machinery. It is the same pleasure a designer feels when one good component solves three problems, or a musician when one motif carries a whole piece. And it feeds perspective-taking: every point on the arc is a different vantage point, yet they all agree on the angle — a small lesson in how different viewpoints can share a truth.

Carry one idea forward: before reaching for something new, ask what the tool you already have can be made to do.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: show you can state, use and justify angles in the same segment.

  • Draw a circle and a chord AB, mark two points P and Q on the same arc, and join them to A and B. State that ∠APB = ∠AQB and give the reason (angles in the same segment).
  • Use the theorem to find a missing angle in a diagram, writing the reason at each step.
  • Explain in your own words why the angles are equal — using the angle at the centre.
  • stretch Describe what changes if P and Q are on opposite sides of the chord (in different segments).
