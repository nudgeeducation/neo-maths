NEO Mathematics: Equations, Inequalities and Simultaneous Relationships

Where two lines meet

Driving Question: When must two relationships both be true — and where is that?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the algebra unit, where solving becomes keeping the balance.

Stepping Stone: interpret the intersection of two lines as the common solution of a pair of simultaneous equations — the single (x, y) that satisfies both — and read it off the graph, checking by substitution into each equation.

You will need: the four operations and their inverses, working with negative numbers, and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed today.

🌿 Connection — Both true at once

🔤 The idea for this lesson

A single line is a whole set of solutions. Now take two lines and ask: which pair (x, y) makes both equations true at once? Each line is that equation's solution set, so a pair on both lines must be where they cross. That crossing point is the common solution — the answer to the two equations taken together, or simultaneously.

What to do: two lines are drawn. Read where they cross, enter that point, then watch it substituted into both equations to prove it satisfies each.

What you are trying to notice: only one point lies on both lines. Points on just one line satisfy only that equation; the crossing satisfies both — that is what "common" means.

🚶 Movement — Slide across to the meeting

What to do: step the vertical line left and right. At each x the two lines give two y-values. Hunt for the x where the two y-values become equal — that is where the lines meet.

What you are trying to notice: away from the crossing the two lines disagree (two different y-values for the same x); at exactly one x they agree. That shared (x, y) is the common solution.

↔️ Where do the two agree?

Carry this forward: the meeting is the one x where both relationships give the same y.

🪞 Reflection — On both, or just one?

What to do: a point is marked against two lines. Decide whether it is the common solution (on both lines) or not. The tool substitutes it into each equation to check.

What you are trying to notice: a point on just one line satisfies only that equation; only the crossing satisfies both. "Common solution" means "makes both true" — and that is exactly the intersection.

✅ Does it satisfy both?

Carry this forward: the common solution is the point that lies on both lines — nothing else does.

🎨 Creativity — The break-even point stretch

What to do: two plans cost different amounts as the hours (or miles) grow. Each is a line. Where they cross is where they cost the same — the break-even point. Find it, and see which plan is cheaper on each side.

What you are trying to notice: a crossing is not just geometry — it is a decision point. Before it, one plan wins; after it, the other. The common solution is where they are equal.

💷 When do the plans cost the same?

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

Two separate rules, each with endless solutions of its own — and yet there is a single place where they agree completely. There is something satisfying in that: out of two whole lines of possibility, one shared point. Take a breath and enjoy the neatness of it.

Notice one thing: did "solve two equations at once" feel less daunting once you saw it was simply "where do the two lines cross?" Just notice the shift from algebra to a picture and back.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment here, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the power of two conditions at once — the everyday reasoning behind "which deal is better, and when?" A single point that satisfies two demands together is one of the most useful ideas in mathematics, from choosing a phone plan to balancing a design against a budget. Seeing it as a crossing makes an abstract "solve these together" suddenly concrete.

Carry one idea forward: when two rules must both hold, look for where they meet — that shared point is the answer to both.

🛠️ Final Task — Evidence for today

Your task: find the common solution of two equations from where their lines cross.

  • Plot two lines on the same axes (for example y = 2x − 1 and y = x + 1) and read the point where they cross.
  • Check the crossing by substituting it into both equations — it must make each one true.
  • Test one other point that is on only one line, and explain why it is not the common solution.
  • stretch Set up a break-even question of your own (two cost plans) and find where they cost the same.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a plotted graph with the crossing marked and checked, a short spoken explanation, or a poster of a break-even decision.
