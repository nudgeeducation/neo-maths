NEO Mathematics: Number Systems and Calculator Judgement

Fractions, decimals and percentages fluently

Driving Question: How many ways can one number be written?

Lesson type: Core — the opening lesson of the unit. One number, many forms.

Stepping Stone: move fluently between fractions, decimals and percentages; order numbers given in different forms; and calculate with fractions and mixed numbers, including fractions of amounts.

🌿 Connection — The linked number line

🔤 The idea for this lesson

3/8, 0.375 and 37.5% look different, but they are one number — one point on the number line. A fraction, a decimal and a percentage are three ways of writing the same position. Choosing which to use depends on what you need it for.

What to do: choose how many equal parts each whole is cut into (the denominator), then move the point. Its fraction, decimal and percentage change together. Work through the tasks below the line.

What you are trying to notice: every position has all three names at once. Some fractions give decimals that stop; some, like 2/3, give decimals that go on for ever.

📍 One point, three names

Parts in each whole:

Carry this forward: a fraction, a decimal and a percentage are three names for one point. To compare numbers in different forms, picture where they sit on the line.

🚶 Movement — Fraction pictures

What to do: each calculation has a picture. Use the buttons to change the picture, then type the answer.

What you are trying to notice: fractions can only be added when the pieces are the same size (a common denominator). Multiplying takes a fraction of a fraction — and the picture shows why you multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators. Dividing asks how many fit — and the picture shows why dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal (the fraction turned the other way up: the reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3).

🧩 See the calculation

Carry this forward: same-size pieces to add; to multiply, multiply the numerators and the denominators; to divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal — because that counts how many fit.

🪞 Reflection — Which form wins?

What to do: first, tap the cards from smallest to largest. Then, for each situation, choose the form that is clearest.

What you are trying to notice: numbers in different forms can be compared once they are in the same form — or placed on the same line. And in real life, one form is usually clearer than the others for the job.

🃏 Order, then choose

Carry this forward: to compare, use one form. To communicate, use the form your reader needs.

🎨 Creativity — Fractions of amounts

What to do: solve each problem. The bar shows the whole split into equal parts. Then write a puzzle of your own.

What you are trying to notice: a fraction of an amount, one amount as a fraction of another, and finding the whole from a part are three views of one bar.

📊 Parts and wholes

✨ Make your own stretch

Write a "find the whole" puzzle like the ticket problem, using a fraction with denominator 5 or 8. Draw its bar on the Scratchpad, and give it to someone to solve.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

One point on a line, three names. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: half a pizza, 0.5 of a pizza and 50% of a pizza are exactly the same amount of pizza. Only the words changed.

You can pause here and return whenever you are ready.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds number sense — knowing where a number sits and how big it is, whatever form it arrives in. Discounts, interest rates, test results and recipes all arrive in different forms. Seeing past the form to the size is a life skill.

Carry one idea forward: when a number looks unfamiliar, ask where it sits on the line.

🛠️ Final Task — One number, many forms

Your task: show that you can move between forms, compare them, and calculate with fractions.

  • Complete a table for 3/5, 0.875, 12.5%, 7/4 and 2/3: give each as a fraction in its simplest form, a decimal and a percentage.
  • Place all five on one number line from 0 to 2, and list them in order.
  • Work out 2/3 + 3/4, 1 1/2 × 2/5 and 2 ÷ 3/8, showing a picture or a method for each.
  • Solve: "3/8 of a field is planted. The planted part is 120 m². What is the area of the whole field?"
  • stretch Explain why 2/3 gives a decimal that never ends, but 3/8 does not.

Submission choices: an annotated Scratchpad image, a photographed page, a short slideshow, or a spoken/video walkthrough.
