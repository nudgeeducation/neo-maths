Recurring decimals as fractions

Driving Question: Can a decimal go on for ever and still be a fraction?

Lesson type: Core — decimals that never end, why they repeat, and how to turn them back into fractions.

Stepping Stone: decide whether a fraction gives a terminating or a recurring decimal; explain why recurring decimals must repeat; write them with dot notation; and convert a recurring decimal to a fraction.

You will need: changing fractions to decimals by division, and simplifying fractions — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator today: it would hide the pattern.

🌿 Connection — Terminate or recur?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Some fractions give decimals that stop: 3/8 = 0.375. These are terminating decimals. Others go on for ever, repeating the same digits: 1/3 = 0.333… These are recurring decimals, written with a dot over the repeating digit: 0.3̇. When a block of digits repeats, dots go over its first and last digits: 0.4545… = 0.4̇5̇. Every fraction gives one or the other — and every recurring decimal can be turned back into a fraction.

What to do: for each fraction, predict: will its decimal terminate or recur? Then see its decimal, and the prime factors of its denominator.

What you are trying to notice: once the fraction is in its simplest form, its denominator decides. Look at the prime factors.

🔮 Predict, then check

Carry this forward: simplify first. If the denominator's only prime factors are 2 and 5, the decimal terminates; otherwise it recurs.

🚶 Movement — Line up the tails

What to do: a recurring decimal is called x. Choose what to multiply it by, so that the two repeating tails line up exactly. Then subtract: the tails cancel, and a fraction appears.

What you are trying to notice: multiply by 10 if one digit repeats, by 100 if two digits repeat, by 1000 if three repeat. When the tails match digit for digit, subtracting removes them completely.

🪞 Reflection — The remainder wheel

What to do: press Next step to divide, one digit at a time. Each remainder lights up on the wheel. Watch for a remainder that comes round again. Then answer the questions.

What you are trying to notice: dividing by 7, the only possible remainders are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. If the remainder is never 0, then within at most 6 steps a remainder must come back — and from then on, every digit repeats. That is why the decimal recurs.

🎡 Watch the remainders

Carry this forward: dividing by d, there are only d possible remainders. So the digits either stop (remainder 0) or must repeat — and the cycle is at most d − 1 digits long.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A decimal that goes on for ever can be captured exactly by two small whole numbers. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: picture the remainders going round the wheel, like the hands of a clock. Sooner or later they must come back to where they have been — and the pattern begins again.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds the joy of certainty about the infinite. You cannot write out every digit of 0.142857142857…, but you can know exactly what it is, and prove why it repeats. That kind of reasoning — about things too big to check one by one — is at the heart of mathematics.

Carry one idea forward: when something goes on for ever, look for the pattern that makes it predictable.

🛠️ Final Task — From for ever to a fraction

Your task: show that you can predict, explain and convert recurring decimals.

  • Without dividing, predict which of these terminate: 3/16, 4/9, 7/20, 5/14, 9/12. Explain each prediction using prime factors.
  • Work out 4/7 by long division. List the remainders, and explain why the decimal must repeat.
  • Write 0.5̇, 0.3̇6̇ and 0.1̇4̇5̇ as fractions in their simplest form, showing the multiply-and-subtract method.
  • Explain, with the method, why 0.9̇ = 1.
  • stretch Write 0.23̇ (that is 0.2333…) as a fraction. Why do you need two multiplications?
