Surds: meaning and simplifying

Driving Question: Why keep √2 instead of writing 1.414…?

Lesson type: Core — roots that are not whole numbers or fractions, why we keep them exact, and how to tidy them.

Stepping Stone: recognise a surd (a root that is irrational); explain why an exact surd is better than a rounded decimal; use √(ab) = √a × √b to simplify a surd using its largest square factor; multiply and divide surds; and add like surds.

You will need: square numbers and factors — and the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator today: exact answers are the point.

🌿 Connection — Exact or rounded?

🔤 The idea for this lesson

√2 is the number which, multiplied by itself, gives exactly 2. Its decimal starts 1.41421356… and never ends or repeats, so it cannot be written as a fraction: it is irrational. A root like this is called a surd. Writing √2 keeps the value exact; writing 1.414 does not.

What to do: choose how many decimal places of √2 to keep. Square the rounded value and see how close it gets to 2. Then sort some roots into exact values and surds.

What you are trying to notice: however many decimal places you keep, the square is never exactly 2.

✖️ Use the rule

Carry this forward: √a × √b = √(ab) and √a ÷ √b = √(a ÷ b). But √a + √b is not √(a + b).

🪞 Reflection — The square-factor finder

What to do: the dots show the number under the √. Try a square number as a factor: if it fits, the dots regroup into equal square blocks. Read off the simplified surd, then type it in its simplest form, like 6√2.

What you are trying to notice: 72 = 36 × 2 means 72 dots make 2 blocks of 6 × 6. So √72 = √36 × √2 = 6√2. The largest square factor gives the simplest form in one go.

🔲 Find the square blocks

Try a square factor:

A picture of √2. Each side of the tilted square is the diagonal of a 1 by 1 square, so by Pythagoras its length squared is 1² + 1² = 2: the side is √2. The tilted square covers 4 half-squares, so its area is 2. Six of these sides end to end make 6√2 — the side of a square of area 36 × 2 = 72.

stretch Why must √a × √b = √(ab)? A pattern suggests — a proof convinces.

Carry this forward: find the largest square factor, take its root outside, and leave the rest inside: √(k²m) = k√m.

🌙 Rest — A pause to let it settle

A decimal that never ends or repeats, held exactly in two symbols: √2. Take a slow breath.

Notice one thing: picture the tilted square on the grid. Its side is a length you can draw perfectly — yet you can never write it down perfectly as a decimal.

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds an appreciation of exactness. A rounded answer is often good enough — but a surd is perfectly right, and stays right however many times you use it. The proof that √2 is irrational is over two thousand years old, and still as true as the day it was found.

Carry one idea forward: keep things exact for as long as you can, and round only at the very end.

🛠️ Final Task — Keep it exact

Your task: show that you can explain, simplify and calculate with surds.

  • Explain why √2 is called a surd, and why we write √2 rather than 1.414.
  • Simplify √45, √98 and √200. For each, name the largest square factor.
  • Work out √6 × √24, √300 ÷ √3 and 2√3 + √27.
  • Explain why √9 + √16 is not √25, but √9 × √16 is √144.
  • stretch Prove that √a × √b = √(ab), or prove that √2 cannot be written as a fraction.
