Direct and inverse proportion

Driving Question: When one doubles, what does the other do?

Lesson type: Core — four relationships, one test: what stays the same?

Stepping Stone: recognise direct and inverse proportion; set them up as y = kx and y = k/x (and with squares and roots); find the constant of proportionality k from one pair of values; and link each to its graph.

You will need: the unitary method (Lesson 1), substitution, and squares and square roots, plus the optional ✏️ Scratchpad. No calculator needed.

🌿 Connection — The Proportion Explorer

🔤 The idea for this lesson

Two quantities are in direct proportion when y = kx: double x and y doubles, y ÷ x is always k, and the graph is a straight line through the origin. They are in inverse proportion when y = k/x: double x and y halves, x × y is always k, and the graph is a curve. k is the constant of proportionality, and one pair of values is enough to find it. The same idea works with squares and roots: y = kx², y = k√x.

What to do: choose a situation. Step x up and down: each value is added to the table and plotted. Then show the curve.

What you are trying to notice: one column of the table never changes. Which one, for each situation? And what does each graph look like?

🍎 Nutrition — What this feeds

Mode: intellectual. The mathematics is the nourishment, so we name honestly what the learning feeds.

What it feeds: today feeds prediction. Knowing the kind of relationship and just one pair of values lets you predict every other value. Many laws in science work in exactly this way, from how a spring stretches to how light fades with distance.

Carry one idea forward: find what stays the same.

🛠️ Final Task — Two relationships

Your task: choose one real situation in direct proportion and one in inverse proportion (for example: buying more of something; sharing a job or a journey at different speeds).

  • For each, make a table of at least four pairs of values and show the test that stays the same.
  • Find k and write the formula.
  • Sketch each graph on numbered, labelled axes.
  • Use each formula to predict a value that is not in your table.
  • stretch Explain why the inverse proportion graph never touches either axis.
