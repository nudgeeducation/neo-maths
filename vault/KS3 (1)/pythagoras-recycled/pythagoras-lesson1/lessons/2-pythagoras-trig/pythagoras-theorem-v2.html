Right Triangles and Hidden Squares

A notation detail that matters

c is not automatically the hypotenuse in every possible labelling. It is the hypotenuse here because angle C is 90° and side c is opposite C.

If the right angle were at A, then side a would be the hypotenuse and the relationship would be b² + c² = a².

The general verbal theorem is therefore: the square of the hypotenuse length equals the sum of the squares of the other two side lengths.

Carry this idea forward: a² + b² = c² represents the Pythagorean relationship only when c is the length of the hypotenuse — the side opposite the right angle.
🪞 Reflection — What evidence makes the formula true for this diagram?

Your task: use the diagram below as your evidence. Work through the six questions in order. You are not being asked to invent a proof from memory — you are being asked to read the geometry carefully and explain what each part tells you.

Read the diagram in this order

  1. Find the small square right-angle marker.
    Which vertex is it at?
  2. Look directly opposite that right angle.
    Which side is there?
  3. Name that side.
    Why is it the hypotenuse?
  4. Use the opposite-side convention.
    Which lower-case letter names its length?
  5. Move from length to area.
    If the side length is c, what is the area of the square built on it?
  6. Compare the three square areas.
    How do a² and b² combine to match c²?

Now join your answers into one explanation

You can use this sentence frame, change it, or explain the same reasoning in your own way:

“The right angle is at vertex ___. The side opposite it is ___, so this is the hypotenuse. The lower-case letter ___ names the length of that side. A square built on a side of length ___ has area ___. The other two squares have areas ___ and ___. The diagram shows that ___ + ___ = ___, so in this labelled right triangle the relationship is a² + b² = c².”

What you are trying to notice: the formula is not true because the letter c is magically special. In this convention, c is used for the hypotenuse length because it is opposite vertex C, where the right angle is located in this diagram.

🎨 Creativity — How can I show the theorem without starting with the formula?

What to do: create a small teaching card for someone who has only memorised a² + b² = c² and does not yet know what it means.

Choose one format: annotated diagram, three-frame comic, short voice-script, metaphor, or a colour-coded Scratchpad page.

Your card must reveal:
  • the triangle must be right-angled;
  • the hypotenuse is opposite the right angle;
  • the theorem compares square areas;
  • c names the hypotenuse length in the standard convention.

What you are trying to notice: a strong explanation can begin with the geometry and arrive at the symbols, rather than beginning with a memorised formula.

🌙 Rest — How can I make the notation small enough to enter?

You do not need to hold the whole diagram and formula in attention at once.

One connection at a time:
  1. Find the right-angle marker.
  2. Find the side opposite it.
  3. Name that side the hypotenuse.
  4. Only then connect its length to c.
  5. Only then read c² as the area of the square on that side.

Pause after any step. The mathematical relationship will still be there when you return.
